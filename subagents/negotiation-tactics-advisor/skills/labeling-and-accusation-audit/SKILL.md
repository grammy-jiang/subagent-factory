# Labeling and accusation audit

## Purpose

Acknowledge and name a counterpart's emotional state before responding to the
content of a negotiation. Two related moves do this work:

- **Labeling** — naming an emotion you observe with an observational opener so the
  counterpart feels seen. Naming a negative emotion drains its intensity; naming a
  positive one reinforces it (PRIN-002, CL011–CL013).
- **Accusation audit** — opening an emotionally loaded exchange by voicing the
  negative assumptions the counterpart is likely to hold about you or the deal,
  before they raise them, so the objection is defused in advance and the
  counterpart tends to reassure you rather than attack (PRIN-003, CL015–CL016).

Both are emotional-anchoring tools: they set the counterpart's emotional state
*before* numbers or terms enter the conversation (CL030).

## When to use

- The counterpart shows an emotional signal — frustration, hesitation, enthusiasm,
  resistance — that has not been acknowledged.
- The counterpart needs to feel heard before they will engage with a proposal.
- You are about to present an offer, a difficult request, or a price, and the
  counterpart is likely sceptical or hostile: lead with an accusation audit to
  pre-empt the objection.

Do **not** run an accusation audit when the counterpart holds no negative
preconception — manufacturing one introduces suspicion that was not there. Skip a
label when tone and content are already aligned and no unspoken emotion is present,
or when a misread emotion would create more confusion than silence would.

## Procedure

### Labeling

1. **Read the emotion.** Identify the dominant feeling the counterpart is signalling
   through tone, word choice, or hesitation.
2. **Name it as an observation, not an assertion.** Use an opener that frames it as
   something you notice: "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…".
   Avoid "I" framing ("I think you're…"), which shifts focus onto you.
3. **Deliver the label and stop.** Go silent immediately and let the counterpart
   respond. Filling the silence undercuts the label (CL014).
4. **Let the response steer.** A negative label that lands lowers intensity and the
   counterpart becomes more cooperative; a positive label amplifies goodwill.
   Follow whatever they reveal.

### Accusation audit

1. **List the negatives.** Before the conversation, write down every harsh thing the
   counterpart could think or say about you, your position, or the deal.
2. **Voice the worst ones first, using label framing.** Open by naming them yourself:
   "You're probably thinking…", "It may seem like…". Anchor emotion before you
   introduce any number (CL030).
3. **Pause for the correction.** The counterpart will typically move to soften or
   disown the accusation, reassuring you rather than attacking — that reversal is
   the audit working.
4. **Only then introduce content.** With the emotional ground cleared, present the
   offer, request, or terms.

## Inputs

- A description of the counterpart's current emotional signals and stance.
- The negative assumptions the counterpart is likely to hold about you or the deal.
- The stage of the negotiation (pre-offer framing vs. mid-conversation) — the audit
  belongs at the framing stage, before the emotional anchor is set.

## Output

- One or more concrete labels phrased with observational openers, each followed by
  a deliberate silence.
- For a loaded opening: a short accusation-audit script naming the likely negatives
  in the counterpart's own anticipated words, delivered before any numbers.
- The one-sentence tactical-empathy reason each move fits the situation.
