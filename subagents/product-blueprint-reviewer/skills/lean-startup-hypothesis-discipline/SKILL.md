# Lean-Startup Hypothesis Discipline

## Purpose

Check a product blueprint's hypotheses, probing evidence, and validation or convergence claims
against lean-startup hypothesis discipline. The discipline applies to early-stage, uncertain
business ideas that are structured around explicit assumptions, canvas-style scaffolding, and
customer or stakeholder probing (P004). Its grounding is the empirical study of 152 NSF I-Corps
teams (Katila & Leatherbee, 2020), which tests how hypothesis formulation, probing, and
convergence actually relate to one another and how team composition bends that relationship, plus
one experiment-taxonomy principle on matching probe type to uncertainty. Use this skill to keep a
blueprint's evidence claims honest about what probing can and cannot establish, and to keep its
hypothesis set workable rather than sprawling.

## When to use

- The blueprint states explicit assumptions or hypotheses about the business idea, product, or
  users — a canvas-style breakdown of value proposition, segments, channels, or similar areas —
  under genuine, unresolved uncertainty (P004).
- The blueprint cites customer or stakeholder interviews, prototypes, concierge tests, Wizard-of-Oz
  tests, or concept tests as evidence for a decision.
- The blueprint claims an assumption is "validated," "confirmed," "proven," or that the idea has
  "pivoted" or "converged," and that claim needs checking against what the evidence can actually
  support.
- The blueprint describes, or implies, a team whose composition — MBA-heavy, single-discipline, or
  educationally diverse — could shape which assumptions get probed and how the resulting evidence
  is read.
- The blueprint proposes a recurring or repeatable discovery cadence across teams, releases, or
  time periods.

This skill does not judge blueprint altitude, build-trap output-thinking, gap classification, MVP
staging, or downstream stage routing — those belong to the sibling review skills. It is also not a
fit when the blueprint contains no explicit hypotheses, assumptions, or probing evidence at all;
there is nothing here to check.

## Procedure

1. **Confirm the applicability gate.** Lean-startup hypothesis discipline is for early-stage
   business ideas with unresolved uncertainty, worked through explicit assumptions, canvas
   scaffolding, and customer or stakeholder probing (P004). If the blueprint's claims are already
   settled facts with no live uncertainty, this skill's checks are not the bottleneck.

2. **Check that probing — not paperwork — is doing the learning.** Interviews and other probes
   should be shown doing real work: reducing uncertainty on existing assumptions, feeding
   convergence, and also surfacing hypotheses the team had not previously considered; probing can
   dislodge a team from its starting idea, not just rubber-stamp it (P005). Flag a blueprint that
   asserts "we talked to N users" without any resulting change to assumptions, scope, or
   direction.

3. **Check hypothesis count and stopping discipline.** Assumptions should be crisp and moderate in
   number. Once an assumption converges — the team is confident enough to stop treating it as
   open — the blueprint should show hypothesis generation slowing for that area, not an
   ever-growing, uncapped backlog of untested claims (P002). Flag a sprawling assumption list with
   no visible convergence or stopping signal.

4. **Check that assumptions are made visible before probing is chosen.** The blueprint should
   expose its underlying business-model assumptions structurally — a canvas-equivalent breakdown
   such as value proposition, customer or user segments, channels, and resources — so that what
   gets probed is a deliberate choice, not an afterthought (P028). Treat the blueprint's stated
   vision or value proposition as one of these hypotheses too: it should read as something meant to
   emerge from and be shaped by experimentation, describing capabilities rather than locking a full
   feature set, not asserted as already-decided (P015). Flag a vision or value proposition
   presented as settled fact with no assumption trail behind it.

5. **Check the epistemic strength of probing language.** Customer and stakeholder interviews are
   probes that reduce uncertainty; they are not controlled experiments or final scientific
   validation (P017). Flag any claim that an assumption is "proven," "scientifically validated," or
   "confirmed" on interview evidence alone — the defensible claim is that uncertainty was reduced
   enough to stop treating the item as an open hypothesis.

6. **Check that the probe type matches the uncertainty.** Different assumptions call for different
   tools: concierge (manual and open, tight feedback loop, fits early B2B work, does not scale),
   Wizard of Oz (looks finished to the customer, manual backend, gathers feedback at scale),
   concept testing (a landing page, wireframe, or prototype — usually generative), and
   flow-testing prototypes used only after problem-level research has already happened — and in
   every case the objective is learning, not earning (P016). Flag a blueprint that reaches for a
   scaled or earning-oriented test (a built feature, a wide rollout, a formal A/B test) before the
   underlying problem-level uncertainty has been probed at all.

7. **Read team composition into how much weight the evidence deserves.** Assess team composition
   up front, because it changes adoption, interpretation, and change behavior (P008). If the
   blueprint's team is MBA-heavy or otherwise trained in learning-by-thinking and analytical
   frameworks, expect resistance to probing; require that probing was forced first, with the
   team's analytical strength then applied to interpreting the resulting interview evidence before
   it is allowed to support a major change (P001). For technology-driven academic teams
   specifically, treat lean startup as a commercialization method to actively manage rather than
   assume will work, and check that MBA involvement is supporting probing rather than displacing
   it (P122). If the team is educationally diverse, treat that diversity as a resource for broader
   questioning, but verify the blueprint shows it actually converted into stakeholder probing and
   evidence use, not just broader internal discussion (P011). Treat any documented instructor or
   leadership feedback only as a coaching nudge for a visibly under-engaged team, not as proof that
   a team-composition correlation is itself an idea-quality signal (P050). This evidence comes from
   small, technology-driven teams inside one standardized program, so generalize it cautiously —
   temper confidence when the blueprint's team or setting looks materially larger, less technical,
   or less standardized (P009).

8. **Distinguish cosmetic edits from real reorientation.** When the blueprint claims a pivot, a
   validated convergence, or meaningful learning, check whether that claim reflects a major
   business-idea change actually driven by probing evidence, rather than a minor canvas edit
   dressed up as validation (P123). Also check that the claim is backed by qualitative decision
   records — what was decided, and why — rather than resting on an interview tally alone (P124).

9. **Check that progress is tracked as three separate signals, not one blend.** The blueprint, or
   the process behind it, should track hypothesis formulation, stakeholder probing, and convergence
   decisions separately — for example, new assumptions logged per period, interviews conducted per
   period, and items resolved (confirmed as fact, or removed as invalid) per period — rather than
   folding them into one undifferentiated "progress" number (P052). If the blueprint proposes this
   as a recurring program across teams or releases, check that cadence, platform or reporting
   fields, feedback loops, team expectations, and interview targets are standardized enough to keep
   the three signals comparable over time (P013).

10. **Guard against over-reading activity as performance.** If the blueprint, or its supporting
    rationale, treats a high volume of hypotheses, interviews, or "lean-startup activity" as itself
    proof of quality or predicted performance, flag that framing: treat higher activity intensity
    as a promising but exploratory signal only, never as proof of venture or product
    performance (P049).

## Inputs

- The blueprint's stated hypotheses or assumptions (or business-model-canvas-style breakdown), and
  any evidence offered for them: interview counts, prototype, concierge, or Wizard-of-Oz
  descriptions, and any pivot or convergence claims.
- Any description of the team or process producing the blueprint: composition (MBA presence,
  educational diversity, single- versus multi-discipline), and whether validation is a one-off
  effort or a recurring, repeatable program.
- Qualitative decision records, where available, to check against evidence that rests only on an
  interview count.

## Output

A findings entry for each hypothesis, probe, or validation claim reviewed, naming: the principle
applied (P0xx), what the blueprint currently states, the gap or risk if the check fails, and a
concrete remediation — for example, "reframe as reduced uncertainty rather than proven," "add a
stopping signal for this assumption," "name the team's MBA composition and require forced probing
before this counts as a major change," or "add qualitative decision records alongside the
interview count." These findings feed into the reviewer's overall structured blueprint critique
alongside the altitude, build-trap, gap-classification, and routing findings produced by the
sibling skills.

and Hypothesis-Based Probing of Business Ideas" (Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal), an empirical

and the team-composition boundary conditions (P001, P002, P004, P005, P008, P009, P011, P013,

on treating vision as an experiment-shaped hypothesis (P015, P016) draw on this same package's
