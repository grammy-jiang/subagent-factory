# Segmentation and Positioning Playbook

The tables for deciding *who* to sell to and *why they should prefer you*. Use it to redirect
spend away from chasing the whole market and toward the relevant, winnable customers, and to
test whether a brand's positioning actually gives a reason to buy.

## 1. Fish where the fish are — targeting sequence

Most companies do not know who their customers are, so they reach the wrong people and
overspend. Start from who could possibly buy, then slice thinner until the target is a
workable size. Priority order:

| Priority | Group | Action |
|----------|-------|--------|
| 1 | People who already buy your product | Get them to buy more, more often. |
| 2 | The undecided | Give them a concrete reason to buy. |
| 3 | Everyone else | Forget about them — do not spend to convince the whole market. |

Trying to convert everyone who is not already a buyer (the "convince the world" fantasy) is
the classic waste. Keep current customers before chasing new ones.

## 2. The two-group market split

| Group | Definition | Treatment |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| A | People who do, could, or might conceivably use a product in your category | Your fishing waters — segment and target within this group. |
| B | People who will not use the category at all | Out of scope; spend here returns nothing. |

A large audience that sits in Group B (or in a category that will never buy) is the wrong
lake regardless of its size — the test to apply to sponsorships and endorsers.

## 3. Differentiation — three ways to give a reason to buy

A brand is differentiated from a commodity (where price is the only lever) by giving the
customer a clear reason to choose it over competitors. Three basic routes:

| Route | What you tell the customer |
|-------|----------------------------|
| More for more | Pay a premium and get more than you can get elsewhere (the approach of category-leading brands). |
| (Equal/parity plays) | Match the category on the price of entry and win on another relevant difference. |
| Price | Compete on being cheapest — the default when nothing else differentiates (commodity territory). |

Differentiation creates value only when the difference is one customers actually prefer and
will pay for; a difference no one cares about is not positioning.

## 4. Brand adoption building blocks — positioning rubric

Positioning is built in layers. Each must be earned in order; the higher layers are what move
volume.

| Layer | Role | Effect |
|-------|------|--------|
| Price of entry | Required | Must have it to be considered; having it does not help you, lacking it hurts you. |
| Differentiation | Motivating | Begins to separate you from the pack, but on its own lacks the mass appeal to drive meaningful volume growth. |
| Preference | Crucial | Significant point of leverage with target customers; can propel a brand to category leadership. |

**Preference is perishable** — it must be re-earned continuously; competitors copy any
advantage, turning a difference into a mere price of entry. Re-run the targeting and
positioning loop rather than assuming a won customer stays won.

## How to use

- Diagnose a **wrong-market** spend with tables 1–2.
- Diagnose a **weak-positioning / no-reason-to-buy** spend with tables 3–4.
- Feed the right-customer test into `sponsorship-decision-review` and
  `endorser-fit-assessment`.
