# Endorser Fit Assessment

## Purpose

Judge whether a celebrity spokesperson, endorser, or icon will actually sell more product —
not merely attach a famous face to the brand. A big name is no guarantee that customers
line up, and the wrong fit can damage the brand. This skill tests an endorser choice against
the one legitimate reason to hire one: transferring relevant associative imagery that makes
the target customer buy.

## When to use

- A company is deciding whether to hire a celebrity, athlete, or icon as spokesperson or
  endorser, or which of several candidates to pick.
- A brand is paying for a big name on the assumption that fame alone will drive sales, and
  the caller needs that assumption challenged.
- An existing endorsement produced attention but no measurable selling lift, or carries
  reputational risk, and needs review.

## Procedure

1. **Confirm the only valid reason: borrowing relevant equity.** The legitimate reason to
   hire a celebrity is to borrow the image and values consumers already associate with them
   and transfer that associative imagery to the brand — because that association is what gets
   people to buy. Reject reasons that amount to grabbing attention or fame for its own sake.
2. **Test whether the brand even needs borrowed equity.** If the brand already conjures the
   images and associations you want, you do not need to rent them. If it does not, identify
   exactly which qualities must be borrowed so the customer makes the connection.
3. **Test relevance of the celebrity's qualities to the brand.** What matters is that the
   spokesperson's qualities are relevant to the brand, not that the name is big or expensive.
   A modest or even non-celebrity choice can outperform a megastar (e.g. an owner-as-spokesman
   or a non-human icon) when the associations fit. Reject "big name" as sufficient on its own.
4. **Test relevance to the target market — per segment.** The celebrity who works in one
   market can flop in another. When selling to more than one customer type, check fit for each:
   different segments admire different figures (e.g. older buyers favour consistent, rule-
   following performers; younger buyers favour edgier, independent ones; young children respond
   to animated characters). Cross-check segments with `references/segmentation-and-positioning-playbook.md`.
5. **Weigh the downside risk.** A big-name endorser is no guarantee of sales and sometimes
   damages the brand. Name the failure modes — the celebrity overshadowing the product, an
   off-brand or risky association, or reputational exposure — and judge whether the expected
   selling lift justifies that risk.
6. **Tie the choice to a measurable selling outcome.** Require that the endorsement be judged
   on whether it sells, not on recognition or buzz. Hand measurement design to
   `advertising-results-audit`.

## Inputs

- The endorser candidate(s), the brand and the associations it currently does or does not
  evoke, the target market(s) and their segments, the fee, the proposed message, and the
  selling outcome the endorsement is meant to produce.

## Output

A fit verdict per candidate: whether the celebrity's qualities are relevant to the brand and
to each target segment, whether the brand actually needs borrowed equity, the downside risk,
and a recommendation tied to a measurable selling outcome — naming the preferred choice and
the condition under which a rejected one would win.
