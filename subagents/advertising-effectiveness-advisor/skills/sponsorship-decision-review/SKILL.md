# Sponsorship Decision Review

## Purpose

Judge a proposed or renewing sponsorship — an event, team, property, or naming-rights
deal — by the only question that matters: will it sell more product, or merely buy logo
visibility? Sponsorship is the fastest-growing and most financially risky form of
advertising, and most sponsors spend without knowing why. This skill forces the decision
through a selling-accountability gate before any check is cut, and through a quantitative
return test before any deal is renewed.

## When to use

- A company is deciding whether to sponsor an event, team, or property, or whether to
  renew an existing sponsorship.
- A sponsorship produced attendance, eyeballs, or logo placement but no measurable selling
  result, and the caller needs a critique of why and what to demand instead.
- A property seller is pushing terms, and the sponsor needs to know whether those terms
  serve the sponsor's selling goal or only the seller's.

## Procedure

1. **Establish why you are sponsoring.** Reject "we always have" and "our competitors are."
   These produce white elephants — rights bought in the heat of the moment that deliver
   nothing but irrelevant logos on a stadium wall. If there is no selling reason, stop here.
2. **Name the selling outcome the sponsorship must produce.** Sponsorship is not about free
   tickets, schmoozing, or capturing eyeballs. Reframe the goal away from viewers, attendees,
   or participants and toward increased sales and brand equity. State the concrete result
   (more units, more often, to more buyers, at a better price) the deal is meant to drive.
3. **Test relevance to the real customer.** Confirm the property reaches the buyers who do,
   could, or might use the product — not just a large crowd. A big audience that will not
   buy is the wrong lake. Cross-check against `endorser-fit-assessment` when the property is
   built around a person.
4. **Check the balance of power with the property seller.** Property sellers will steer the
   deal toward promoting the property, not the sponsor's brand. Identify every term that
   serves the seller's interest over the sponsor's selling goal, and flag where the sponsor
   risks being railroaded into spending that sells the event rather than the product.
5. **Plan the activation, not just the rights fee.** The rights fee is the tip of the
   iceberg; companies typically spend several times more to advertise, promote, and implement
   a sponsorship. Require a plan for how the property will be *leveraged* to carry a message
   that sells the brand. A deal with no activation plan fails the gate.
6. **Define the measurement before signing.** Decide what success means (incremental sales,
   loyalty, purchase intent) and set a baseline. Plan to compare pre- and post-sponsorship
   sales, or sales in a region where the property is leveraged against a region where it is
   not, and to divide total property-utilization spend by the incremental sales change to get
   a cost-per-incremental-sale figure to compare against the usual cost-of-sales.
7. **Never renew without measuring the prior term.** If an existing deal was never measured,
   that is itself the finding: treat renewal as blocked until a return figure exists. Hand
   the measurement design to `advertising-results-audit`.

## Inputs

- The sponsorship or renewal under question, the property and its audience, the sponsor's
  brand and current selling situation, who the real and target customers are, the proposed
  rights fee and activation budget, the seller's terms, and the selling result the spend is
  meant to produce (with any baseline or prior-term data).

## Output

A structured verdict on whether the sponsorship is accountable to selling: the selling
outcome it must produce, which of the standard sponsor mistakes are present (no reason, no
goal, ceded power, no activation, no measurement), and a prioritized set of corrections — or
a pass/fail gate on a renewal tied to the measured return of the prior term.
