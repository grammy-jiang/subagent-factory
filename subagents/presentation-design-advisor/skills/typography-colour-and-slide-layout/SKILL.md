# Typography, Colour And Slide Layout

## Purpose

This skill sets the readable surface of the slide before the content on it is judged on its own terms. It starts by treating the presentation software's defaults — type size, typeface, background, palette, logo placement — as an unvalidated inheritance rather than guidance, since none of it was founded on research and all of it is baked into institutional templates, and it assesses every default against the actual audience, purpose, and occasion instead of following it or avoiding the software altogether. It forces a large minimum type size, because small type comes from not knowing the material well enough or believing more text is more convincing, and the floor compels the presenter to find and explain only the most salient points. It chooses the typeface by reading speed rather than tradition, because the audience reads while listening: sans serif faces read faster in the short blocks of a slide, their straighter strokes matter most for viewers at a sharp side angle, and a serif face's penalty becomes decisive once the projector or room lighting is poor. It boldfaces slide type, especially for a larger room, and avoids italics and underlining, both of which read too slowly or add noise once projected. It fixes the background colour before the rest of the palette, choosing dark or light from the formality of the event and the venue size, then verifies the whole palette by projecting it in the actual room rather than trusting the authoring screen. It opens blank space between elements so the slide does not crowd, blur the viewing order, or scatter the audience's attention, holds text blocks, headlines, and lists to firm limits, uses animation only where it serves the audience, and keeps any logo secondary to the visual evidence.

## When to use

- Type size, weight, or typeface is being chosen or reviewed for a projected deck, especially for a large room or poor projection conditions (P007, P098, P049).
- A background or full colour palette needs setting, or a palette that looked fine on the authoring screen must be checked once it will actually be projected (P099, P011).
- A slide feels crowded, its viewing order is ambiguous, a headline or list runs long, or the audience visibly bounces between elements (P004, P023).
- An institutional template or brand rule is driving the design — a default master, a mandated logo on every slide — without anyone having weighed it against this audience and room (P017, P008).

## Procedure

1. Treat the presentation software's defaults for type, colour, background, and logo placement as an unvalidated inheritance rather than guidance, since none of it was founded on research and all of it is baked into institutional templates, and assess each one against the actual audience, purpose, and occasion, proactively changing what does not serve them rather than following the defaults or avoiding the software (P017).
2. Force a large minimum type size, because small type comes from not knowing the material well enough or believing more text is more convincing, and let the size floor compel the presenter to find and explain only the most salient points (P007).
3. Choose the typeface by reading speed rather than tradition, since the audience reads while listening: favour sans serif faces, which read faster in the short blocks of a slide and whose straighter strokes matter most for viewers at a sharp side angle, and treat a serif face's penalty as decisive whenever the projector or room lighting is poor (P098).
4. Boldface the slide type, especially for a larger room, and avoid italics — too slow to read once projected, particularly from side angles — and underlining, whose underscore adds noise that impairs letter recognition (P049).
5. Fix the background colour before setting the rest of the palette, choosing dark or light from the formality of the event and the venue size, because pure black and pure white afford the greatest contrast while mid-range coloured backgrounds render parts of a palette unusable (P099).
6. Whenever the deck will be projected, verify the resulting colour palette by projecting it in the actual room rather than judging it on the authoring screen, checking contrast against the background and among the chosen colours and tweaking manually while projecting, because contrast that looks adequate on the author's monitor can be entirely unreadable once projected, and bring your own projector or arrive early enough to adjust the venue's (P011).
7. Open blank space between a slide's elements, since too little does three separate kinds of damage — the slide feels crowded, the viewing order becomes ambiguous, and the audience bounces inefficiently between elements — and watch for the default master causing it by reserving obsolete transparency-era gutters and by setting text larger than it needs to be (P004).
8. Set the layout to guide the eye by holding text blocks and especially headlines to no more than two lines, holding any list or set of call-outs to two, three, or four items, using animation only where it serves the audience and never gimmicky, and leaving enough blank space between elements that the slide breathes (P023).
9. Keep any logo secondary rather than putting one on every slide, even where brand rules mandate a logo on every slide, by identifying the institution and presenter on the first and last slides and on any distributed slide, placing a logo in a bottom corner rather than the upper-left space the assertion headline claims, and removing any logo that crowds the visual evidence (P008).

## Inputs

- The deck or template, the room and projection conditions, any brand or institutional template constraints, and the audience's viewing angles and distance.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. ## Anti-patterns to flag

- Slides packed edge to edge with no breathing room between elements, so the eye cannot tell which item to read next and the viewing order feels arbitrary (P004).
- Body text small enough that the presenter has to read it aloud rather than point at it, or a slide carrying more lines than anyone past the front rows could resolve (P007).
- A logo stamped in the same corner of every single slide, sized or placed so it overlaps or crowds the chart, photo, or figure beside it (P008).
- A palette that looks crisp on the laptop screen but turns muddy or low-contrast the moment it reaches the venue's projector, with no evidence anyone checked it there beforehand (P011).
- A deck still carrying the software's out-of-the-box master — default bullet indents, default title placement, default colour scheme — with no sign anyone weighed it against this audience, purpose, or room (P017).
- Headlines running three or more lines, lists stretching past four items, or a slide with no discernible path for the eye because nothing on it was arranged to lead attention (P023).
- Body claims or emphasis set in italics or underlined text that goes soft, blurs, or reads slowly from the back rows or at a side angle instead of being boldfaced (P049).
- A serif typeface carrying the deck's body text, its strokes visibly dropping out or blurring once the room dims or the viewing angle sharpens (P098).
- A mid-toned or saturated coloured background that swallows half the chosen palette into near-invisibility, rather than a deliberate pure black or pure white fixed before the rest of the palette (P099).
