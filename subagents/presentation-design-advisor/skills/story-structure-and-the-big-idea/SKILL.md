# Story Structure And The Big Idea

## Purpose

This skill settles what the presentation is about and in what order it unfolds, before any slide exists. It writes the big idea as a complete sentence that articulates the presenter's unique point of view and what is at stake, because a topic is not a big idea and addressing it to "you" is better still. It keeps presentation software closed during idea generation so that content is collected and created first, and it makes quantity the explicit objective of ideation, expecting the genuinely clever ideas to surface only in the third or fourth round rather than the first. It settles the story on separate sticky notes — the main message and each supporting assertion — arranged into the order that tells the best story and treated as the argument that carries the audience to the main message, after which any detail supporting none of them is excluded. It opens by stating what is, a concise formulation of what everyone agrees is true now, because accurately capturing the audience's reality proves the presenter understands their context and supplies the baseline without which the new idea has no dramatic effect. It makes the turning point an explicit, memorable call to adventure delivered at the moment the contrast becomes stark, and it lands the ending on a higher plane than the beginning. It uses stories for two specific jobs — creating anticipation and supplying a recall frame — while keeping the talk a blend of document and story, and it keeps a personal catalogue of stories indexed by emotion for when a specific feeling is wanted at a specific point.

## When to use

- The answer to "what is this presentation about?" comes out as a noun phrase rather than a sentence with a point of view, so the controlling message needs to be rewritten (P060).
- A new presentation is starting: ideation must precede slide-building, with quantity as the objective and the software left closed until content exists (P005, P118).
- The story itself needs settling before slides are built, by sorting the main message and its supporting assertions into the order that tells the best argument (P035).
- The arc needs shaping: the what-is baseline, the turning point that makes the contrast stark, and an ending on a higher plane than the beginning (P087, P116, P059).
- Stories are being chosen or written into the talk, or a specific emotion is wanted at a specific point (P041, P064).

## Procedure

1. Write the big idea — the single controlling message — as a complete sentence that articulates the presenter's unique point of view and conveys what is at stake, doing this whenever the answer to "what is this presentation about?" comes out as a noun phrase such as a quarterly update, because a topic is not a big idea and addressing it to "you" is better still, since that ensures the idea is addressed to someone (P060).
2. Before opening presentation software for a new presentation, collect and create content first, keep generating ideas along a theme until possibilities are exhausted, and expect the genuinely clever ideas to appear only in the third or fourth round rather than the first (P005).
3. Throughout the ideation phase of presentation development, make quantity the objective: generate many ideas in any form — words, diagrams, scenes, literal or metaphorical — and keep going even when they wander, rather than stopping at the first idea, because stronger solutions typically surface only after four or five have percolated (P118).
4. Settle the story before opening the slide software by writing the main message and each supporting assertion on separate sticky notes, arranging them into the order that tells the best story, and treating that arrangement as the argument — the steps that carry the audience to the main message — after which any detail supporting none of them is excluded (P035).
5. When about to propose something new, open by stating what is — a concise formulation of what everyone agrees is true about the current or historical situation — because accurately capturing the audience's reality proves the presenter understands their context and values, creates a common bond, and supplies the baseline without which the new idea has no dramatic effect (P087).
6. When the audience must be moved from complacency into wanting a different reality, make the call to adventure an explicit, memorable big idea conveying what could be, delivered at the moment the audience first sees the stark contrast with what is, ensuring the turning point is never muddled or vague since everything that follows exists only to fill that gap (P116).
7. When writing the final movement of a persuasive presentation, land the ending on a higher plane than the beginning by repeating the most important points and then describing with wonder and awe the world that exists once the idea is adopted, showing the reward is worth the effort and asserting the idea is not merely possible but the right and better choice (P059).
8. Use stories for two specific jobs — creating the anticipation that makes an audience lean in, and supplying the frame that lets them recall the sequence of details hours later — while keeping the talk a blend of document and story rather than a story outright (P041).
9. When a specific emotion is wanted at a specific point in the presentation, maintain a personal catalogue of stories indexed by emotion, recalling them not only by life chronology but by people, places and things, sketching the scene to surface dormant memories, and recording how the presenter felt as each one unfolded (P064).

## Inputs

- The occasion, the audience, what the presenter wants to change, the raw material available, and how much preparation time remains.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- The stated purpose of the talk is a noun phrase — a topic, a subject area, a quarterly update — rather than a sentence asserting a specific point of view, and no one is named as the "you" the idea is addressed to (P060).
- The deck already contains fully built slides before any idea list or outline exists, and the record of the ideation phase shows the presenter stopped generating alternatives after the very first idea (P005).
- The idea-generation record shows only one or two ideas captured in a single form before the presenter moved to building slides, rather than a wide set of ideas in multiple forms explored until they stopped producing anything new (P118).
- The talk's structure was assembled directly in slide software with no separate list of the main message and its supporting assertions, and the outline contains details that trace back to none of them (P035).
- The talk launches straight into the new idea or proposal with no stated baseline of what the audience already believes is true, leaving the audience without the common ground needed to feel the contrast (P087).
- The moment meant to be the turning point is buried in a subordinate clause or scattered across several slides, so no single moment marks where the audience should feel the contrast between what is and what could be (P116).
- The final slide or closing lines simply restate the opening, or trail off on a note no higher than where the talk began, with no picture of the improved world once the idea is adopted (P059).
- The talk is either a flat sequence of bullet-point facts with no narrative pull at all, or it reads as a story from start to finish with no factual backbone the audience could recall unaided (P041).
- The presenter has no bank of personal stories to draw on and instead searches for an anecdote on the spot when one is needed, with no record of which emotion each remembered story carries (P064).
