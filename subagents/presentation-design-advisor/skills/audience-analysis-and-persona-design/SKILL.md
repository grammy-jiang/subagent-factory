# Audience Analysis And Persona Design

## Purpose

This skill establishes who the talk is for and builds the design around them. The audience is the hero and the presenter the mentor, so what they leave holding is the measure rather than how impressive the presenter appeared. Use it to build a persona per segment, test each detail against understanding and interest, handle jargon and mixed audiences, and calibrate how much emotion and evidence this room will take.

## When to use

- A presentation is being started and the audience has not yet been characterised: the seven
  audience questions are unanswered and no persona exists per segment (P061).
- Draft slides or messaging read as being organised around the presenter's own expertise or agenda
  rather than around what the audience needs and will do (P056, P058).
- Draft copy or messaging carries jargon or acronyms aimed at people outside the presenter's field
  or department (P057).
- The audience is mixed in technical level or background, or is unknown to the speaker (P013,
  P075).
- The balance of proof and emotional appeal must be tuned for an analytical or an emotionally
  driven audience, or a talk did not land and the cause may be the audience read (P119, P120,
  P063, P114).
- A deck's styling or polish appears to be tracking the presenter's own image rather than the
  clarity of the message (P074).

## Procedure

1. Before building any slide, answer the seven audience questions and build one persona per
   distinct audience segment, keeping each persona slide at the front of the deck as private
   working context that is never projected to the room (P061).
2. Build the presentation around what the audience needs and will do rather than around the
   presenter's agenda, since they came to find out what the presenter can do for them and their
   needs are the benchmark for checking the message (P056).
3. For any presentation whose outcome depends on the audience acting, cast the audience as the
   hero and the presenter as the mentor: defer to them, make each member feel addressed
   personally, and judge success by what they leave holding rather than by how impressive the
   presenter appeared (P058).
4. When reviewing draft slide copy or messaging, or when an expert addresses a mixed or
   nonspecialist audience, never assume the audience has kept up with the presenter's field,
   because specialised jargon and acronyms aimed at nonspecialists reduce the help and funding an
   idea attracts purely because listeners do not understand it, and this holds even inside one
   organisation where departments trade in different languages and acronyms (P057).
5. When planning what the audience takes away, discharge the mentor's two duties — teaching and
   gift-giving — by supplying important, useful, previously unknown information plus guidance,
   confidence, and tools, so the audience leaves knowing something new they can apply (P114).
6. For a known audience, test each detail of the talk against two questions — will they understand
   it, and will they be interested in it — and when the audience is unknown, mingle beforehand to
   ask what work people do, why they came, and what they know, or else try the talk on someone
   with the same background (P075).
7. When the audience mixes technical levels or is mixed in background and interest, abandon the
   goal of satisfying that mixed audience throughout the whole talk — no design achieves it — and
   aim instead for everyone satisfied by the end, addressing the different audiences at different
   moments (P013).
8. When tuning the amount of emotional or evidentiary material for a specific audience, calibrate
   to its tolerance in both directions: analytical audiences in fields such as economics, science,
   engineering, or research read heavy heartstring-tugging as manipulation yet are still motivated
   by showing how lives will be changed, while emotionally driven audiences want to know the
   details were considered but need only a few proof points rather than twenty slides of them
   (P119).
9. When reviewing where a presenter's material naturally sits, locate it on the head-heart-gut-groin
   spectrum and correct in whichever direction the presenter defaults: move analytical
   communicators lower because many decisions are emotional, and move purely emotional
   communicators higher because they otherwise lose analytical audiences for lack of proof and
   damage their own credibility (P120).
10. When diagnosing why a talk did or did not land, check it against three traits rather than one —
    content worth the audience's time, visible passion for it, and a keen sense of who the audience
    is, what they already know, why they are listening, and what preconceptions they hold — since
    strong content can carry an unconventional delivery, but no delivery rescues a talk aimed at
    the wrong audience (P063).
11. Refuse to treat the slides as an extension of the presenter's persona: keep the top concern on
    how well the message communicates rather than on how impressive the deck looks, since polish
    neither guarantees meaning nor substitutes for a missing strategy, and stage fear — which ranks
    above the fear of dying — is not answered by prettier slides (P074).

## Inputs

- Who will be in the room, what they already know, why they are there, what preconceptions they
  hold, and what the presenter wants them to do afterwards.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to
  achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual
trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact
first, and mark where the source's guidance is conditional or context-bound rather than presenting
it as settled. This skill advises on presentation design and delivery; it does not write the talk,
build the deck, produce the graphics, deliver the presentation, or rule on whether the underlying
result is correct.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A deck arrives with no upfront record of who the audience segments are or what the seven
  audience questions established, or a persona slide sits inside the body of the deck where it
  would be projected to the room instead of being kept as private working notes (P061).
- The talk's structure visibly tracks the presenter's own topic list or career narrative, with no
  sign that content was chosen for what the audience needs to do afterwards (P056).
- Slides and script refer mainly to the presenter's expertise, credentials, or accomplishments,
  with the audience cast as spectators of the presenter's competence rather than as the one who
  benefits and acts (P058).
- Slide copy or spoken script carries specialised jargon or department-specific acronyms with no
  definition, aimed at listeners outside the presenter's field or organisation (P057).
- A talk delivers only description or reassurance with no new, useful information the audience can
  take away and apply, or supplies information with no accompanying guidance, confidence, or tool
  for using it (P114).
- A plan for a known audience skips checking whether each detail will be understood and found
  interesting, or a plan for an unknown audience proceeds straight to slide-building with no
  attempt to learn beforehand who will be in the room (P075).
- A single version of a mixed-audience talk is designed to hold every segment's full attention for
  its entire length, with no moments addressed to one segment while another waits (P013).
- A talk aimed at an analytical audience such as engineering or research piles on heartstring
  appeals with no proof points, or a talk aimed at an emotionally driven audience buries them in a
  long run of proof slides with no acknowledgement of the human stakes (P119).
- A presenter's material sits entirely at one end of the head-heart-gut-groin spectrum with no
  correction toward the other end, regardless of which way the presenter naturally defaults (P120).
- A retrospective on a talk that failed blames delivery alone, without checking whether the content
  was worth the audience's time, whether passion for it was visible, or whether the audience itself
  was misjudged (P063).
- A deck is polished or stylised in ways that track the presenter's own image or brand rather than
  the clarity of the message, or nervousness about presenting is treated as solvable by adding more
  visual polish to the slides (P074).
