# Format Choice And Preparation Planning

## Purpose

This skill decides whether there should be a live presentation at all, in what format, and how much work it deserves. The decision starts by weighing a presentation against a circulated document or written report on five advantages — fielding questions, reading the audience's expressions, delivery emphasis, richer visual aids, and evidenced receipt — against five disadvantages: one pass only, no time for the audience to look things up, the audience held to the speaker's pace, success hostage to delivery, and the difficulty of assembling everyone. Where that comparison shows the content really is a document, it changes the format rather than the slides: hold a meeting, circulate the document beforehand or let the audience read it at the start and spend the rest of the time on discussion and action plans, or, if the goal is only to convey facts and figures, cancel the meeting and send a report. Once a live talk is confirmed, it constrains the length hard, because short attention spans make constraint the thing that forces concision. It picks a specialised format only on its stated conditions — the Lessig style for keynotes and after-dinner talks reused on multiple occasions, pecha kucha where a manager or instructor must raise a group's presentations quickly with no time to teach a more sophisticated structure. It scales preparation time to the stakes and books the cycles as soon as the engagement is known. It briefs illustrators with the story to be told rather than with what to draw. And it judges the finished talk by whether the audience looks enlightened, moved to act, or willing to change behaviour.

## When to use

- The communication channel is still open to choice — a live presentation, a circulated document, or no meeting at all (P009, P031).
- A talk's length or a specialised format, such as the Lessig style or pecha kucha, is being chosen for the occasion (P088, P027, P051).
- Preparation time must be scaled and booked against a known delivery date, or work is being handed to an illustrator or designer (P065, P062).
- Success criteria for the finished talk are being defined ahead of delivery (P010).

## Procedure

1. Before deciding whether a presentation is the right channel at all, weigh it against a circulated document or written report by comparing five advantages — fielding live questions, reading the audience's expressions, using delivery for emphasis, richer visual aids, and evidenced receipt — against five disadvantages: one pass only, no time for the audience to look things up as they could in a document, the audience held to the speaker's pace, success hostage to how well the delivery goes, and the difficulty of assembling everyone in one place; do this whenever the channel is still open to choice or the medium itself needs justifying (P031).
2. Where that comparison shows the content really is a document — because it cannot be reduced to a visual aid or the goal is only to convey facts and figures — change the format rather than the slides: hold a meeting but circulate the document beforehand or let the audience read it at the start and spend the remaining time on discussion and action plans, or cancel the meeting outright and send a report instead (P009).
3. Once a live talk is confirmed as the right channel, constrain its length hard regardless of format, because short attention spans mean the constraint itself is what forces the presenter to be concise and cut anything superfluous — the world's most influential talks land in eighteen minutes or less, and a fixed format such as twenty slides at twenty seconds each forces the same ruthless self-editing (P088).
4. Within that length constraint, reserve the Lessig style — short phrases and images flashed for about a second each in synch with the speech — for keynotes and after-dinner talks that will be delivered on multiple occasions, because audiences read it as light-hearted even though one 15-minute talk can run past 300 slides and demands continual practice to hold the timing (P027).
5. Use pecha kucha instead — twenty slides at twenty seconds each on automatic advance — specifically where a manager or instructor must raise a whole group's presentations quickly and there is no time to teach a more sophisticated structure, accepting that the rigid window still demands substantial preparation to compress each scene's speech and that some scenes, such as a graph, simply do not fit it (P051).
6. Scale how much preparation time the talk receives to its stakes and book that time as soon as the engagement is known, treating roughly 36 to 90 hours as the working range for an hour-long 30-slide talk, reserving from-scratch development for the high-stakes occasions, and allowing only minimal reuse of existing material for the low-stakes ones (P065).
7. When part of the preparation is handed to a professional illustrator, brief them with the story to be told rather than with what to draw, give them time for research, ideation, and revision — or accept a quick sketch when time is short and the vision is already clear — trust their expertise over untrained subjective opinion, and consolidate every stakeholder's feedback into one non-conflicting direction before it reaches them (P062).
8. Once the talk is delivered, judge it by whether the audience looks enlightened, moved to act, or willing to change behaviour, and get there by reviewing the content through their eyes, showing them something they have never seen before, and combining strong stories with convincing information delivered humanly (P010).

## Inputs

- The occasion and its stakes, who must receive the content, the time available before and during, and what the institution's templates and norms already impose.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first, and mark where the source's guidance is conditional or context-bound rather than presenting it as settled. This skill advises on presentation design and delivery; it does not write the talk, build the deck, produce the graphics, deliver the presentation, or rule on whether the underlying result is correct.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A live meeting is scheduled for material that is only facts and figures, with no companion document circulated and no discussion or action-item agenda attached to it (P009).
- A post-mortem on a talk records only what the speaker did or said, with no check on whether the audience looked enlightened or moved to act and no fresh insight the audience had not already seen (P010).
- A one-off internal update is built as 300 rapid-fire Lessig-style slides demanding constant timing rehearsal, for a talk that will only ever be given once (P027).
- A recommendation to hold a live meeting cites none of the audience-facing advantages, such as fielding questions or reading the room, and ignores the reassembly cost or the one-pass risk, so no side of the trade-off is weighed (P031).
- The rigid twenty-slides-at-twenty-seconds pecha kucha format is applied to a data-heavy talk whose graphs need lingering discussion, or to a team that already has ample time to be trained on a fuller structure (P051).
- A brief to the illustrator is a literal drawing instruction with no story context, no time budgeted for revision, and separate unreconciled feedback arriving from each stakeholder (P062).
- A high-stakes external pitch has no preparation time booked until days before delivery, or a low-stakes internal update is rebuilt from scratch instead of reused (P065).
- A submitted deck runs well past eighteen minutes' worth of material with no forced cut, and nothing in its format compels self-editing (P088).
