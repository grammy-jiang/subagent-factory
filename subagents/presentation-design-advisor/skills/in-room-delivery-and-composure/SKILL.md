# In-Room Delivery And Composure

## Purpose

This skill governs what the speaker does while the talk is running, from taking the room in hand before starting to holding steady through whatever happens once it does. It takes charge of the room as part of delivery — bringing a remote advancer if the speaker means to walk, clearing furniture from the walking space, and adjusting lights or shades — because a passive presenter ends up on the wrong side of the screen or projecting washed-out slides and is the one credited or blamed for the result. It replaces the question of how to handle nervousness with the question of how to achieve confidence, whose answer is passion combined with preparation, supported in the moment by focusing on the science rather than on the audience. It requires three things of the speaker in a strong presentation: understanding the subject well enough that what is imparted is worth the audience's time, showing genuine enthusiasm for it, and holding a keen awareness of what the audience knows, what will engage them, and what biases they carry. It directs the audience's attention with the eyes on a workable rule — meeting everyone's eyes before the end in a small room, looking several times at every section in a large one — and rejects the myth of looking above their heads at the back wall. It plans several deliberate delivery-mode changes within an hour, because media-conditioned audiences begin to squirm within about ten minutes of an unvaried speaker. It holds composure through anything mid-talk, since audiences judge a speaker only on how they react to an alarming event, not on the event itself. It accepts that no single delivery style is correct and asks the speaker to develop one they are actually comfortable giving. And it judges a delivery only against the audience and room the speaker actually faced, since a familiar audience invites a warmth that a hostile or distant one does not.

## When to use

- Coaching or critiquing a speaker's style, including one that violates conventional rules of dress, eye contact, or stance (P016).
- The speaker must take charge of the room, direct eye contact deliberately, or vary delivery mode across a long slot (P106, P107, P111).
- The speaker is nervous, or something goes wrong mid-talk — a technical failure, an interruption, or an unrelated disturbance — and composure is the issue; this skill covers general mid-delivery mishaps, nerves, and room control, whereas composure under a hostile question or a direct challenge belongs to questions-challenge-and-composure (P110, P108).
- Judging or defending a delivery's warmth or formality against the audience and room the speaker actually faced (P066).
- Assessing whether the speaker shows command of the subject, genuine enthusiasm, and awareness of the audience (P079).

## Procedure

1. Take charge of the room before the talk begins — bringing a remote advancer if the speaker intends to walk, clearing furniture that blocks the walking space, and adjusting lights or shades — because a passive presenter ends up on the wrong side of the screen or projecting washed-out slides and is the one credited or blamed for the result (P106).
2. Replace the question of how to handle nervousness with the question of how to achieve confidence, whose answer is passion combined with preparation — passion alone only a source of it — supported in the moment by focusing on the science rather than on the audience (P110).
3. Require three things of the speaker in a strong presentation: understanding the subject well enough that what is imparted is worth the audience's time, showing genuine enthusiasm for it, and holding a keen awareness of what the audience knows, what will engage them, and what biases they carry, while accepting that no speaker knows everything or needs exceptional charisma, only that they instil a respect for the subject (P079).
4. Direct the audience's attention with the eyes, applying the workable rule of meeting everyone's eyes before the end in a small room and looking several times at every section in a large one, since the advice to look above their heads at the back wall is a myth that teaches the speaker nothing (P107).
5. Plan several deliberate delivery-mode changes within an hour — alternating media, multiple presenters, interaction, movement, a dramatic gesture — because media-conditioned audiences begin to squirm within about ten minutes of an unvaried speaker and the key to holding attention is having something new happen continually (P111).
6. Hold composure through anything mid-talk, since an alarming event may have nothing to do with the talk and audiences do not hold a speaker responsible for failed bulbs, fire alarms, or other listeners' behaviour — only for how the speaker reacts — so that even devastating criticism immediately before speaking leaves one correct move, which is to go up and do the best possible with the cards dealt (P108).
7. Develop a style the speaker is actually comfortable giving, accepting that no single delivery style is correct, since a poised professional manner and an unpolished but passionate one both hold audiences, and a speaker who violates every prescribed rule of dress, eye contact, and stance can still distinguish themselves through sincere passion, deep knowledge, and sensitivity to the audience (P016).
8. Judge a delivery only against the audience and room the speaker faced, since a familiar smiling audience invites movement, storytelling, humour, and vocal variation while an unfamiliar or hostile one naturally produces a more businesslike manner, and a large tiered hall imposes a formal barrier that approaching the front rows only partly bridges — so criticising a speaker's warmth without accounting for the audience is unfair (P066).

## Inputs

- The room layout, equipment, and control the speaker has over it; the length of the slot; the speaker's own delivery style, subject mastery, and state of preparation; and any mid-talk disruption or mishap actually observed or anticipated.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. This skill advises on presentation design and delivery; it does not write the talk, build the deck, produce the graphics, deliver the presentation, or rule on whether the underlying result is correct.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- The speaker arrives to find furniture blocking the walking path, no remote advancer despite planning to move, or fights washed-out slides against uncontrolled ambient light (P106).
- The speaker frames pre-talk nerves as a problem to suppress rather than building confidence through preparation, or dwells on the audience's judgement instead of the material itself in the moments before going up (P110).
- The talk reveals thin command of the subject, flat or absent enthusiasm, or content pitched with no sense of what the audience already knows or is biased toward (P079).
- The speaker's gaze drifts to the floor, out a window, or fixes above the audience's heads at the back wall for the whole talk, leaving whole sections of the room never met eye to eye (P107).
- The same delivery mode runs unbroken for the full hour with no change of media, presenter, movement, or interaction, and the audience visibly disengages around the ten-minute mark (P111).
- A failed bulb, an alarm, a heckler, or a hostile remark from another listener throws the speaker off balance in a way clearly disproportionate to what actually happened (P108).
- The speaker is coached toward a generic "correct" style — a fixed stance, dress code, or eye-contact pattern — regardless of what they are actually comfortable giving (P016).
- A delivery is criticised for being too businesslike or too reserved with no account of whether the audience was unfamiliar, hostile, or seated in a large tiered hall (P066).
