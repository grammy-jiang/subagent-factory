# Rehearsal And Memorisation

## Purpose

This skill governs how the talk is prepared and practised before the day, stopping short of the room itself. It starts by walking through the whole presentation beforehand, even in a mumble, because that rehearsal smooths the transitions between assertions, works out the explanations of difficult concepts, and above all dispels the fear of standing before an audience with nothing to say (P020). It practises toward a no-notes standard by memorising the talk's organisation rather than its wording, using the test that the speaker already knows what the next slide is before advancing to it (P052). That practice is done with the actual slides in front of the speaker, so the visual evidence alone becomes what triggers each thing said — which is what produces delivery that reads as natural rather than recited (P072). Memorising the actual words is kept the exception rather than the standard, reserved for an unusually compressed slot or a short high-stakes fragment such as introducing a colleague or the opening of a difficult talk, while the standard stays a practised but extemporaneous talk delivered with the confidence of knowing and loving the subject (P054). At ordinary presentation length that exception cannot become the rule: a fifteen-minute talk runs past two thousand words at a normal speaking pace, a memorised result cannot be changed mid-stream, and it recalls words faster than an audience can absorb them, so memorising the whole talk is rejected outright (P095). Reading from a script gets the same treatment by default, since it runs too fast for comprehension, fixes the speaker's eyes on the page so neither party can read the other, and leaves the audience wondering whether the speaker actually knows the subject (P105). Finally, the risk of groping for a word mid-talk is answered with repeated practice rather than with a script, on the reasoning that a scientific audience does not expect an actor's fluency and that a properly spaced pause reads as emphasis rather than as failure (P094).

## When to use

- A rehearsal plan is being built or reviewed for a talk that has not yet been given (P020, P052).
- The speaker intends to work from a script or from memorised text, or is unsure when memorising exact words is legitimate (P054, P095, P105).
- The slides are finished and the speaker needs to learn to speak from the visual evidence rather than reading it or reciting from memory (P072).
- The speaker is worried about losing a word or a train of thought mid-talk and is considering a written script as the fix (P094).
- The question is about preparing and practising the talk before the day: general mid-talk nerves, room control, eye contact, pacing, and delivery-style judgment belong to in-room-delivery-and-composure instead.

## Procedure

1. Walk through the whole presentation beforehand, even in a mumble, because the practice smooths the transitions between assertions, works out the explanations of difficult concepts, and above all dispels the fear of standing before an audience with nothing to say (P020).
2. Practise until the talk can be given with no notes by memorising its organisation, applying the test that the speaker already knows the next slide before advancing to it (P052).
3. Rehearse with the actual slides so the visual evidence alone triggers what to say, since this is what produces delivery that reads as natural rather than recited (P072).
4. Keep memorising the actual words the exception rather than the standard, reserving it for an unusually compressed slot or for a short high-stakes fragment such as introducing a colleague or the opening of a difficult talk, while the standard remains a practised but extemporaneous talk delivered with the confidence of knowing and loving the subject (P054).
5. Reject memorising the whole talk at ordinary presentation length, because a fifteen-minute talk runs past two thousand words at a normal speaking pace, the memorised result cannot be changed mid-stream, and it recalls words faster than an audience can absorb them (P095).
6. Treat reading a speech as costly by default, since it runs too fast for comprehension, puts the speaker's eyes on the page so neither party can read the other, and leaves the audience wondering whether the speaker knows the subject (P105).
7. Answer the risk of groping for a word with repeated practice rather than with a script, since a scientific audience does not expect an actor's fluency and properly spaced pauses actually emphasise key points, reserving the slide or discreet notes for wording that genuinely must be exact, such as a law or a difficult definition, without otherwise abandoning extemporaneous delivery (P094).

## Inputs

- The talk as it currently stands, the speaker's familiarity with the material, the slot length, and how much rehearsal time remains before the day.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A rehearsal plan that jumps straight to slide-by-slide wording without ever having walked the whole talk through end to end, so transitions and the harder explanations are still being worked out live in front of the audience (P020).
- A speaker who still has to check notes or the deck to know what comes next, rather than being able to advance from memory of the talk's own structure (P052).
- A rehearsal done reading from a laptop or a printed deck rather than facing the actual presentation slides, so delivery leans on recall of a script instead of on what the visual evidence itself prompts (P072).
- Ordinary passages of the talk committed to memory word-for-word with no compressed slot or high-stakes fragment to justify it (P054).
- A rehearsal plan built around memorising an entire normal-length talk verbatim, rather than around extemporaneous delivery from a memorised structure (P095).
- A full written-out script that the speaker intends to read from at the lectern, rather than notes or slides used to speak extemporaneously (P105).
- A written safety script kept as the fallback for the moment the speaker might lose a word, instead of further rehearsal being the fix (P094).
