# Visual Evidence, Analogies And Graphics

## Purpose

This skill chooses and sequences the visual evidence a slide shows, and sets the limits on analogy and credit that keep that evidence honest. It starts by fixing exactly what is being asserted before any graph or image is chosen, because an audience believes an argument far more readily once it knows and appreciates the assertion the details are driving toward (P001). From that assertion it picks the type of visual evidence that fits the kind of claim being made — a map and photographs for a background fact, photographs with a timeline and graph for a trend, a diagram for a process, a line or bar graph for a quantitative relationship (P034). Where a slide still carries a bulleted list, it replaces the list with the visual form of the relationship the list actually holds — a timeline, call-outs on an image, a pie graph, a diagram — because a list only presents details and leaves the audience to work the connection out for itself (P002). When the main assertion sits too far up the mountain for a single graph or dataset to reach, it builds a chain of evidenced sub-assertions, fits each sub-assertion's evidence to its own kind of claim, and supplies the background warrants the audience needs before it can appreciate the assertion at all (P091). It anchors any concept the audience does not already know in a specific physical example or analogy rather than an abstract or mathematical treatment, because a concrete image is what carries most listeners through a mathematical stretch (P042), and it confines that analogy to conveying how something works, how large it is, or how likely an unfamiliar event is — never to supporting an assertion in an argument, since an analogy ties two dissimilar things from one narrow perspective that a sceptical audience can attack from any other (P078). It then governs the mechanics of display: using animation to control the order a complicated visual is taken in without ever starting a slide's projection from an empty body (P100), weighing Prezi's zoomable single canvas against the extra learning time and lost focused-scene animation it costs (P103), and requiring a short, visible reference listing on every slide that carries another group's photograph, drawing, or graph (P102).

## When to use

- Before any graph or image is chosen for a slide, to fix the exact assertion the data must support (P001).
- A slide already carries data or a technical result and needs the visual-evidence type matched to the kind of claim it makes (P034).
- A slide is built as a bulleted list where the items actually hold a chronological, spatial, proportional, or process relationship (P002).
- The audience is not yet positioned to accept the main assertion from a single dataset, so a chain of sub-assertions and warrants is needed (P091).
- A concept is unfamiliar to the audience, the talk contains an abstract or mathematical passage, or an analogy is doing the work of evidence in an argument (P042, P078).
- A visual carries several simultaneous connections and needs staged reveal, a zoomable canvas is proposed, or a slide reuses another group's photograph, drawing, or graph (P100, P103, P102).

## Procedure

1. Fix exactly what the slide is asserting and confirm the main assertion the data supports before deciding how to graph or present it, because the audience believes an argument far more readily once it knows and appreciates that assertion and every detail shown should then drive toward it (P001).
2. From that assertion, choose the type of visual evidence it must support — a map and photographs for a background fact, photographs with a timeline and graph for a trend, a diagram for a process, a line or bar graph for a quantitative relationship (P034).
3. Where the slide still shows a bulleted list, replace it with the visual form of the relationship the list actually holds — a timeline for chronological details, call-outs around an image for spatial ones, a pie graph for relative sizes, a diagram for a flow — rather than leaving the audience to work the relation out (P002).
4. When the main assertion sits too far up the mountain for one graph or dataset to reach, build a chain of evidenced sub-assertions, fit each sub-assertion's evidence to its own kind of claim, and supply the background warrants without which the audience cannot appreciate the assertion even while following every word (P091).
5. Anchor any concept the audience does not already know in a specific physical example or analogy rather than an abstract or mathematical treatment, because a concrete image is what carries most listeners through the mathematical stretches and can even expose errors that an algebra-only listener would miss (P042).
6. Confine that analogy to conveying how something works, how large it is, or how likely an unfamiliar event is, and never let it stand in to support an assertion in an argument, because an analogy ties two dissimilar things from one narrow perspective that a sceptical audience can attack from any other angle (P078).
7. Use animation to hold back a second visual element until the first has been explained and to reveal a complicated visual piecemeal so the audience follows one connection at a time, but never start the slide's projection from an empty body (P100).
8. Weigh Prezi's zoomable single canvas against its real costs before adopting it to explain a whole system divided into parts — accept the extra learning time it demands, accept that it forbids animating details into a focused scene, and confine the motion to zooming rather than the twisting and rotating transitions that physically unsettle audiences (P103).
9. Put a short reference listing — often just an author surname and a year — on every slide that carries a photograph, drawing, or graph from another group's work, sized so that it need not be legible to everyone but must be visible to everyone, because failing to acknowledge that contribution is at minimum an insult and is more typically regarded as theft (P102).

## Inputs

- The assertion the slide must support, the data or material available for it, the audience's familiarity with the concept, and the provenance of any borrowed figure.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first, and mark where the source's guidance is conditional or context-bound rather than presenting it as settled. This skill advises on presentation design and delivery; it does not write the talk, build the deck, produce the graphics, deliver the presentation, or rule on whether the underlying result is correct.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A slide's graph or image type was picked before anyone could say in one sentence what the slide is arguing, so the details on it never converge on a single point (P001).
- A background fact is carried by a bar graph, or a quantitative relationship is carried by a lone photograph, so the visual form does not match the kind of claim the slide is making (P034).
- A slide is a column of bullet points describing a timeline, a spatial layout, or a set of proportions, with no timeline, image call-out, or pie graph anywhere on it (P002).
- A single chart is asked to carry the whole main assertion in one leap, with no intermediate sub-assertions and no background fact supplied that the audience would need to appreciate the leap (P091).
- An unfamiliar concept is introduced straight into equations or abstract description with no physical example or analogy offered to carry the audience through it (P042).
- An analogy is used as the actual evidence for a contested claim in an argument, leaving the assertion exposed the moment a listener notices where the comparison breaks down (P078).
- A slide opens with a blank body and builds up from nothing, or a complicated visual with several connections is dumped onto the screen all at once instead of being revealed piece by piece (P100).
- A zoomable single-canvas tool is used for a simple two-part comparison, or its motion twists and rotates the view instead of zooming, or a focused-scene animation is attempted inside it (P103).
- A slide displays a photograph, drawing, or graph plainly borrowed from someone else's work with no author name or year printed anywhere on it (P102).
