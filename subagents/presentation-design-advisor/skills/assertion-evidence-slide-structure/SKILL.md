# Assertion-Evidence Slide Structure

## Purpose

This skill designs and reviews the structure of a technical content slide as a pair of substitutions: a succinct sentence stating the slide's main assertion — a hypothesis, assumption, insight, or result — in place of a phrase headline, and visual evidence in place of the bulleted list, with bulleted lists not occurring at all. It treats the bulleted list as a failure on three simultaneous grounds: it does not show connections, does not reveal hierarchy, and leaves the critical assertions unspecified, while its word count crowds out the graphics. It applies the headline mechanics concretely — no more than two lines, left justified, sentence-capitalised with the period optional, starting in the upper-left corner, set at 28 points, and broken so noun, verb, and prepositional phrases stay together with no orphaned word. It permits the evidence to precede the assertion in the three situations the source names — teaching, a complex assertion, and a skeptical audience — but never permits an assertion projected with no visual evidence at all. It expects the structure's largest effect on preparation rather than delivery, and expects crafting the headline to be the hardest part of the work.

## When to use

- A content slide's headline names a topic instead of stating an assertion, the body is a bulleted list, or a persuasive slide's underlying claim is left implicit (P014, P045, P077, P021).
- The team is drafting or auditing sentence-assertion headlines and needs criteria for which draft is stronger, plus the line-count, justification, capitalisation, point-size, corner-placement, and line-break mechanics (P071, P044, P015).
- A slide is missing visual evidence entirely, or the team is deciding whether to show the evidence before the assertion because the setting is teaching, the assertion is complex, or the audience is skeptical (P069, P046).
- Someone is claiming, arguing, or challenging what the structure actually delivers — comprehension gains, preparation effects, or delivery effects — and the measured scope of that claim needs to be stated correctly rather than asserted (P047, P070, P096).

## Procedure

1. Diagnose a failed persuasive slide by checking whether the assertion is left implicit and the slide body never shows the relationship the argument rests on, since writing the assertion as the headline would itself have driven the speaker to find the right evidence, whenever a slide is intended to support a claim or slides under review failed to persuade (P077).
2. Recognise the ease of bulleted lists as their real danger before replacing one, because a list lets the speaker skip deciding how the details relate, requires only parallel grammar, and amounts to brainstorming dressed as a finished presentation — surface thinking that should be a presenter's last choice rather than their first (P021).
3. Reject the bulleted list on three grounds at once — it does not show connections, does not reveal hierarchy, and leaves the critical assertions unspecified — while also noting that its word count makes reading and listening simultaneously difficult and its position squeezes out the space for graphics (P045).
4. Apply the assertion-evidence structure as a pair of substitutions whenever the slide carries technical content or a technical assertion: a succinct sentence stating the slide's main assertion — a hypothesis, assumption, insight, or result — in place of the phrase headline, and visual evidence in place of the bulleted list, with bulleted lists not occurring at all (P014).
5. Expect crafting the sentence-assertion headline to be the hardest part of the work, since it demands identifying each slide's single most important message rather than naming a topic, and, read in sequence, those headlines should give the bare-bones story of the work for the speaker to flesh out aloud (P071).
6. Judge a candidate headline by which version leads the speaker to prepare a more focused talk with fewer words overall and which better re-orients a listener who missed the transition onto the slide, not by which one is shorter (P044).
7. Once the sentence-assertion headline exists, apply the headline mechanics concretely wherever the slide uses this structure: no more than two lines, left justified, capitalised as a sentence with the period optional, starting in the upper-left corner where Western audiences look first, set at 28 points, and broken so noun, verb, and prepositional phrases stay together with no single word orphaned on the second line (P015).
8. Never project the finished assertion with no visual evidence at all, even though showing evidence before the assertion is legitimate: ensure some image is present to exploit the audience's ability to process a picture while listening, since on a white background a headline-only slide leaves over 80 percent of the screen glaring back at them (P069).
9. When the setting is teaching, or the assertion is complex or controversial for this audience, present the evidence before the assertion in the corresponding one of the three named situations: show evidence under a question headline in a classroom before animating in the answer, delay a complex assertion until the visual evidence has been explained and then leave the slide up for soak time, or withhold a controversial assertion so the evidence lands before the naysayers compose their rebuttal (P046).
10. Expect the structure's largest effect to fall on preparation rather than delivery, because the sentence-assertion headline forces the speaker to identify each slide's most important takeaway and only then select the evidence for it, converging on more effective evidence where a phrase headline commonly does the opposite (P070).
11. Expect assertion-evidence slides to improve delivery in three specific ways — more eye contact because the assertion is absorbed into natural speech, more natural speech because words are fashioned on the spot to explain images, and an appearance of greater confidence because the information plainly comes from the speaker — and note that the audience follows the speaker's gaze to an explained image where the reading-aloud pattern pushes them away (P096).
12. Treat the comprehension advantage of assertion-evidence slides as measured rather than asserted: point to the controlled comparison that held narrated speech constant against a conservative baseline better than common practice, found significantly higher comprehension and retention with the essay test reaching p < .01, and reported a gain corresponding to one and sometimes two letter grades (P047).

## Inputs

- The slide, sequence, or deck under review, plus what each slide is meant to assert, who the audience is, and whether the setting is teaching, reporting, or persuading.
- The reasoning offered for the design or decision under review: what the presentation is meant to achieve, what is on the slides now, and any claim about why the current form works.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first, and mark where the source's guidance is conditional or context-bound rather than presenting it as settled. This skill advises on presentation design and delivery; it does not write the talk, build the deck, produce the graphics, deliver the presentation, or rule on whether the underlying result is correct.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A slide's headline is a noun phrase such as "Test Results" while the body is a list of bullet fragments rather than a single asserted sentence paired with an image, chart, or diagram (P014).
- A sentence-assertion headline runs three or more lines, is centred, is set in all capitals, starts mid-slide rather than the upper-left corner, or breaks after an article or between a noun and its verb so a single word is stranded on the second line (P015).
- The deck defends a bulleted list as a time-saver, revealing that the presenter never worked out how the listed items relate to one another before putting them on the slide (P021).
- A headline is defended as better because it is shorter, with no argument for how it focuses the talk or helps a listener who missed the transition find their place (P044).
- A slide carries a bulleted list whose items show no connection or hierarchy, crowds the assertion out of the text entirely, and leaves little or no room on the slide for a supporting graphic (P045).
- The assertion is revealed first in a classroom, for a complex claim, or before a skeptical audience, so the audience must accept or reject the claim before seeing what it rests on (P046).
- The deck or talk claims assertion-evidence slides simply "work better" without pointing to the comparison against a conservative baseline, the significance level, or the letter-grade-sized gain that grounds the claim (P047).
- A slide shows only the sentence-assertion headline against a blank or near-blank white background, with no image, chart, or diagram for the audience to process while listening (P069).
- The presenter reports spending the same or less preparation time choosing evidence after switching to sentence-assertion headlines, suggesting the headline was written after the evidence was already chosen rather than driving the search for it (P070).
- Headlines read as though they were dashed off in minutes and, read in sequence without the talk, give no bare-bones story of the work — a sign the single most important message per slide was never identified (P071).
- A persuasive slide fails to convince and, on inspection, never states the assertion it was built to support, so the relationship the argument rests on is never shown (P077).
- The speaker reads the headline aloud while facing the screen instead of the audience, showing that the assertion was never absorbed into natural speech generated from explaining the image (P096).
