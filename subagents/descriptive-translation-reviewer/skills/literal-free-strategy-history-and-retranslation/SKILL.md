# Literal-Free Strategy History And Retranslation

## Purpose

This skill reviews a translation against the long strategy debate — Dryden's metaphrase/paraphrase/imitation triad, sense-for-sense versus word-for-word, and the classic ranked prescriptions for good translation — and against retranslation and a work's 'afterlife' dynamics. It checks verse prosody, historical register and archaism, scholarly apparatus, and the accuracy of period detail and intertextual borrowing, always against the source and against rival versions rather than in isolation.

## When to use

- A rendering needs placing on the metaphrase-paraphrase-imitation scale or the literal-free axis.
- A verse translation must map both prosodic systems and its author's own literary idiom rather than transfer a form wholesale.
- Historical distance, archaism, or a scholarly apparatus governs the target register.
- A translation is being read in relation to its rivals, its predecessors, or a work's afterlife.
- The reviewer must confirm the translator's command of the source's period, customs, and realia.

## Procedure

1. Read the translation in relation to the rival versions it reacts to, comparing several independent renderings to triangulate the source and expose each translator's characteristic distortions, rather than assessing it in isolation (P030).
2. Apply Borges's evaluative method: verify the translation against the source itself rather than trusting its paratext's fidelity claims or its popularity, detect interpolation or padding by comparing a suspect passage against several independent versions, treat diction rooted in the translator's own era or culture as a tell of an unfaithful hand, and flag any rendering of scenes rather than words as illustration or rewriting rather than translation (P031).
3. Weigh whether an experimental or creative strategy suits a literary or philosophical text, including Pound's energizing, archaizing, foreignizing idiom that risks quaintness to bring out the source's difference, de Campos's transcreation as recreation of the sign in its full corporeality, and Benjamin's transparent, word-for-word interlinear ideal tempered by Ricoeur's linguistic hospitality (P034).
4. Check that the translator defaulted to sense-for-sense translation over slavish word-for-word rendering that produces absurd, opaque target text, reserved closer word-and-syntax attention for sacred or otherwise sensitive texts, and matched the degree of stylistic polish to the source rather than embellishing a plain original (P036).
5. Before reviewing a verse translation, confirm it was prepared by first mapping both languages' prosodic systems and the foreign literary tradition that shaped the author, fixing consistent renderings for the author's signature recurrent words, adding nothing for the sake of rhyme or meter, and abandoning untranslatable rhyme for unrhymed lines that carry the literal sense with modulations offloaded to footnotes (P045).
6. Reckon with genuine untranslatability rather than defensively dismissing it, and where abusive fidelity is at issue, evaluate the translation through close line-by-line comparison for its concentration on moments of density and intensity, its transformations at the syntactic and discursive levels and not just the semantic, and whether it articulates its own tellingly abusive textual effects (P048).
7. Value the translation for its creative transformation and not fidelity alone, judging it at the decisive, analyzable levels — the concrete retention or suppression of particularities and the movement of the syntax — rather than in the phantasmal extremes of 'the spirit' or 'the letter' (P071).
8. Frame the translation, with Levy, both teleologically as communication and pragmatically as a decision process, checking that each choice narrows a definable, context-derived paradigm of alternatives to a single selection governed by objective linguistic material and the translator's own memory and standards (P077).
9. When no other guidance is given, hold the translation to the classic ranked prescriptions for good translation: full understanding of the sense, command of both languages, avoidance of word-for-word and unusual or Latinate forms, and a natural, eloquent target text that transcribes the ideas in the same style with the ease of the original (P142).
10. Check whether a de-modernizing or anti-anachronism method has smuggled in a new anachronism of its own, and name the actual audience a version serves rather than accepting a claim of neutrality, since even a strict literal ideal privileges some particular audience (P149).
11. Place the rendering on Dryden's metaphrase-paraphrase-imitation continuum as a diagnostic scale, and treat default paraphrase — which keeps the author in view — as preferable to servile metaphrase or free imitation (P157).
12. Where the translation cannot satisfy every criterion at once, confirm the trade-off was resolved by the fixed priority of sacrificing ease of composition before style and style before sense, so that correspondence in meaning outranks correspondence in style (P158).
13. Read the translation as one turn in a work's afterlife, expecting it to date while the original endures and to be superseded by re-translation as its own language grows and renews (P171).
14. Locate the translator's chief obstacle in the dead, conventional register of his own language rather than the source language's difficulty, and confirm the target idiom was forged for this period and author rather than borrowed unexamined from earlier writers' inventions (P172).
15. Check that the target register was chosen by matching proportionate historical distance rather than absolute date, and flag archaizing that misrepresents the proportionate feel of antiquity and turns a serious work into an exercise in quaintness (P173).
16. For scholarly translation, require an 'ugly', unliterary but clear apparatus that prioritizes substance over beauty with copious footnotes, and confirm the work's full apparatus — commentaries, variants, epigraphs, dedications, and canceled material — is rendered in appendices and notes rather than folded into the main text alone (P174).
17. Verify the translator acquired exact information about the source's period, customs, and realia and traced the unacknowledged intertextual borrowings woven into the text, since ignorance of realia (rendering a country-seat as a 'village') or passing off a masked borrowing as the author's own is a major source of error (P177).
18. Synthesize the findings highest-impact first: lead with any misreading traceable to ignorance of source realia or an unrecognized intertextual borrowing, then strategy-scale and register mismatches that misplace the whole rendering, then prosody and apparatus lapses, and close with afterlife and re-translation framing notes.

## Inputs

- The translation, its source's period and form, and any prior or rival versions it reacts to.
- The reasoning offered for the decision under review: the orientation, strategy, brief, and any quality claim made.

## Output

Per finding: name the flaw and the principle it violates, apply the correction, state the residual uncertainty and the trade-off it reflects, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. When the request is an advise or compare rather than a post-hoc critique, apply the same checks forward — in advise mode, a recommendation naming the governing principle(s) and orientation with its residual trade-off; in compare mode, a side-by-side of what each option preserves and costs, ending in a brief-weighted recommendation. This skill reviews a translation, a translation choice, or a translation-studies analysis; it does not produce the finished translation or make the publication decision.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- The review treats the translation as a freestanding achievement, praising or damning it without comparing it against the rival versions it reacts to (P030).
- The review takes a translation's preface or popularity as proof of fidelity and never checks a suspect passage against the source or against independent versions (P031).
- The review dismisses an archaizing, foreignizing, or transcreative rendering as merely careless rather than weighing it as a deliberate experimental strategy for a literary or philosophical text (P034).
- The review lets a plain source text be embellished into ornate target prose, or accepts an absurd, opaque word-for-word rendering of an ordinary passage as fidelity (P036).
- A verse translation pads lines with extra words for the sake of rhyme or meter, or forces the source's verse form onto the target without ever mapping the two languages' prosodic systems (P045).
- The review waves away a genuinely resistant passage as untranslatable without close line-by-line comparison of its syntactic and discursive transformations (P048).
- The review scores the translation purely on word-for-word accuracy and never asks whether it draws on the target language's own literary tradition (P071).
- The review treats a translation choice as arbitrary rather than as one move in a chain of decisions that narrows a definable set of alternatives to a single selection (P077).
- The target text reads as a word-for-word transcript riddled with unusual or Latinate constructions instead of a natural, eloquent rendering of the full sense (P142).
- The review credits a de-modernizing or anti-anachronism method as neutral, without asking which particular audience the resulting version actually serves (P149).
- The review calls a rendering 'literal' or 'free' without placing it on the metaphrase-paraphrase-imitation scale or defaulting to paraphrase as the baseline (P157).
- Where sense, style, and ease conflict, the review lets a sacrifice of meaning pass uncriticized because the style or ease was preserved (P158).
- The review treats a translation as a fixed, timeless equivalent of the original rather than one dated version in an ongoing history of retranslation (P171).
- The review blames the source language's difficulty for a flat rendering instead of the translator's dead, conventional target-language register (P172).
- The target register is anchored to the source's absolute historical date rather than to a proportionate historical distance, producing quaint archaism instead of period feel (P173).
- A scholarly translation's apparatus is thinned to a few notes, or the author's commentaries, variants, epigraphs, and canceled material are silently dropped instead of appearing in appendices and notes (P174).
- The translation renders a period detail or realia term generically (a country-seat flattened to a 'village') or passes off a masked intertextual borrowing as the author's own words (P177).
