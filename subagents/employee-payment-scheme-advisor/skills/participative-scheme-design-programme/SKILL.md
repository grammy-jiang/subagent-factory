# Participative Scheme Design Programme

## Purpose

Run the staged management-team programme for designing and installing an employee incentive
payment scheme through participation, so that productivity improvement is produced by
involving people at all levels and sections — and the payment system rewards that improvement
rather than being relied upon to cause it.

The programme operationalises the source's finding that consulting and involving employees
in decisions affecting how they work leads to the best results and can produce dramatic
improvements in company performance. Involvement and consultation at the design and
implementation stages helps management understand contextual variables, surfaces areas
where performance can be improved independent of the scheme itself, and minimises the
scheme's drift and decay over time.

## When to use

- Managers are about to introduce or redesign an incentive or performance-related payment
  scheme and need a sequenced, participative process so the scheme improves productivity
  rather than being resisted or ignored.
- A company facing poor quality, high costs, or possible closure wants to use involvement
  and consultation around its payment system to rally the workforce.
- A management team needs a structured programme of preparatory work before participative
  teams are set up and staffed.

## Inputs

- A description of the payment or incentive scheme in question: its purpose and its current
  or proposed form.
- The levels and sections of staff affected, how their work is interdependent, and the
  productivity outcome sought.
- A statement of where senior commitment to a programme of change currently stands.

## Procedure

### Phase 1 — Establish the three prerequisites

1. **Confirm top-level commitment.** The first requirement is commitment to a programme of
   change made at the top of the organisation. Verify this commitment is real and not merely
   nominal before any further step is taken; without it the scheme will lack the authority to
   make its intended impact.

2. **Build a prepared management team.** The second requirement is a team of managers who
   understand what is being asked of them and have the enthusiasm to make it work. Because
   managers are being asked to change how they reach decisions, they must themselves be
   involved in deciding how to set up the system — this is why forming and preparing the
   management team is the essential first step. Run the `participative-working-groups` skill
   to constitute and task this team.

3. **Plan to convince and assist the workforce.** The third requirement is that the rest of
   the workforce be convinced the project is worth supporting, shown how to make it work, and
   assisted in its operation. This is addressed in Phase 3 below.

### Phase 2 — Senior management discussion

4. **Hold a senior management discussion about the programme.** Senior managers discuss the
   programme and identify the management team to develop it further. At this stage, recognise
   explicitly that participation is difficult to restrict to just one set of decisions: a
   participative approach requires a change of management style and the establishment of a
   standing structure of participation — not a one-off consultation exercise.

5. **Fix productivity improvement as the central focus; keep pay in its place.** Because the
   aim of an incentive scheme is to improve productivity, the key focus of the participative
   process must be ways of improving productivity. This should be linked to a payment system
   capable of rewarding those improvements when they are achieved. State plainly that the
   payment system does not lead to improved productivity; it can only reward productivity
   improvement and make co-operation with improvement efforts easier to secure.

### Phase 3 — Programme of work for the management team

Work through the following four areas with the management team before participative teams
are operational (source: `payment-systems-and-20260612115310-h0011`):

6. **How to operate through participative teams.** Managers discuss what operating through
   participative teams will mean in practice: what difference it will make to their own
   decision-making responsibilities; why they should open up decisions for prior discussion
   with those affected; how the approach differs from what they already do; and what the
   effects on their authority will be.

7. **How to handle proposals from teams.** Managers discuss how to cope with proposals
   arising from teams: the need to respond promptly and demonstrate that things get done; the
   need for flexibility and willingness to adjust plans to suit others; the need to encourage
   team members to prepare and research proposals adequately; and the need to review how much
   change has actually been made in response to team proposals.

8. **Identify which areas each team will represent.** Managers identify the areas of the
   company each team will cover and form initial views on which areas to start with first.

9. **Identify suitable tasks for each team.** Managers analyse company priorities and future
   plans, then select one, and preferably two, tasks for each team. Tasks should require a
   reasonable amount of work and discussion, with a time-scale in the range of one to six
   months; longer projects may be divided into stages. Each manager takes a leading part in
   identifying tasks for teams in his own areas and prepares suitable remits with the
   back-up information needed to proceed.

### Phase 4 — Discussions with staff and workers

10. **Convince staff they want to take part.** The first step with the workforce is
    convincing them that they wish to participate. Because the approach gives employees the
    opportunity to influence decisions affecting their work, a well-prepared presentation of
    the proposals should win their support. Ensure trade-union representatives understand the
    project, how it has worked elsewhere, and how they can play a role — nothing in the
    approach runs counter to their aims, and their support greatly assists acceptance by
    their members.

11. **Constitute teams and appoint joint chairmen.** Invite the workforce or staff to
    nominate or volunteer to make up a team for their area (around six per team) and ask them
    to select one of these people as their joint chairman. The management team assigns the
    supervisor joint chairman and confirms willingness to accept the role.

12. **Brief all joint chairmen.** Hold discussions with all joint chairmen covering what the
    teams should do; procedures and remit; how to chair a meeting; how to write a report;
    efficient administration of meetings; effective communications within and outside the
    team; how the company operates (systems, procedures, manufacturing methods, economics);
    and any other issues the management team considers important or the joint chairmen
    request.

### Phase 5 — Co-ordinator, piloting, and links to pay

13. **Recruit a full-time co-ordinator.** Once most people in the company understand the
    exercise and the level of commitment behind it, call for a volunteer to be seconded for
    one or two years as a full-time Participative Team Co-ordinator, responsible for ensuring
    teams operate smoothly and are adequately serviced.

14. **Pilot, then extend — hand to `pilot-and-extension` skill.** Set teams into operation
    as pilots and extend to other areas. Support this with a six-monthly review (presenting
    progress to all joint chairmen, management, and relevant trade-union representatives) and
    a co-ordinating subcommittee of four or five sufficiently senior people who meet each
    team's joint chairman monthly to discuss progress, problems, and achievements.

15. **Link teams to the payment system with the simplest possible mechanism.** Make it one
    of the teams' tasks to determine how their section's performance can best be measured and
    related to payment under a new performance-related incentive scheme. Devise the simplest
    possible mechanism and links. The extra pay is a reward for the extra effort and improved
    performance the participating teams have produced — it is not what motivates the
    workforce to improve efficiency. The driving force comes from good management and the
    involvement of everyone in a participatory programme of performance improvement.

## Output

A sequenced participative design-and-implementation plan that:

- names and verifies the three prerequisites (top commitment, prepared management team,
  workforce engagement);
- fixes productivity improvement as the central focus and positions the payment system as the
  reward mechanism rather than the motivator;
- records the management team's completed programme of work across all four areas (operating
  through teams, handling proposals, area coverage, task identification);
- schedules staff discussions, joint-chairman briefings, co-ordinator recruitment, piloting,
  six-monthly review, co-ordinating subcommittee, and payment-system links; and
- documents the simplest possible mechanism tying pay to the productivity gains produced by
  participation.
