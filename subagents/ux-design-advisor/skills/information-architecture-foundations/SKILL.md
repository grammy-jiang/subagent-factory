# Information Architecture Foundations

Structure a product's information environment — organization schemes, hierarchy, labels, taxonomies,
metadata, and cross-channel structure — from users, content, and context rather than generic best
practice or competitor copying.

## When this applies

- Designing or evaluating a product's high-level structure, first-level navigation, labeling, or cross-channel identity (P006).
- Creating labels where no ideal controlled vocabulary already exists (P007).
- Creating textual or iconic labels for navigation, headings, links, or index terms (P011).
- Creating or revising a label set rather than a single isolated label (P012).
- Choosing an IA solution, especially when the right answer depends on ownership, metadata, channel constraints, business goals, or user variation (P017).
- Auditing, designing, or explaining what counts as information architecture (P019).
- The same information environment appears across web, mobile, device, voice, or other channels (P028).
- Designing a hierarchical or top-down organization structure (P030).
- IA recommendations affect content creation, governance, templates, metadata, or partner distribution (P032).
- Defining the scope or role of information architecture work (P042).
- Creating categories, labels, or taxonomies for contested, abstract, or stakeholder-owned content (P043).
- A controlled vocabulary needs richer semantic relationships than preferred and variant terms alone (P045).
- Maintaining or redesigning an information environment that must evolve over time (P070).
- An information environment will be maintained, extended, or governed after launch (P072).
- Choosing among alphabetical, chronological, geographical, topical, or other organization schemes (P073).
- A large information environment contains mixed content types, structured collections, or associative relationships (P074).
- A product depends on metadata-driven search, browse, indexing, or cross-system consistency (P075).

## Procedure

Apply these principles to the situation under review; for each, name the user need at stake and the trade-off the choice carries.

1. Treat information environments as places: use semantic cues, ordering, labels, typologies, and familiar concepts to shape users' understanding of where they are and what they can do. (P006)
2. Generate candidate labels from content analysis, extraction tools, authors, SMEs, user advocates, card sorting, free listing, and search logs, but validate them before treating them as final. (P007)
3. Design labels as compact representations that use user language, distinguish destinations, avoid organizational jargon, and are tested in their actual channel context. (P011)
4. Design labels as a scoped, consistent system by managing style, presentation, syntax, granularity, comprehensiveness, audience terminology, and modular boundaries. (P012)
5. Analyze each architecture problem through users, content, and context instead of applying generic best practices or competitor patterns uncritically. (P017)
6. Evaluate IA through its component systems: organization, labeling, navigation, search, content/task elements, and invisible metadata or vocabulary systems. (P019)
7. Design cross-channel coherence from abstract semantic structures that can be expressed differently in each channel while remaining recognizable and consistent. (P028)
8. Begin architecture exploration with a hierarchy, then balance mutual exclusivity, cross-listing, breadth, depth, and expected growth through user testing. (P030)
9. Include content-management implications in strategy work, aligning IA with content strategy, rules, roles, resources, templates, metadata, and thesauri. (P032)
10. Frame architecture work as technology-independent design of information environments, not as guidance tied to one site, device, software package, or job title. (P042)
11. Design organization and labeling with awareness that classification shapes understanding, reflects perspectives, and carries organizational politics. (P043)
12. Design thesauri as semantic networks of equivalence, hierarchy, and associative relationships, using professional judgment and research rather than expecting one correct design. (P045)
13. Preserve stable semantic structures while allowing controlled adaptation, separating fast-changing areas from slower architectural foundations. (P070)
14. Plan for IA maintenance after launch because content, people, and context keep changing and the architecture must evolve intentionally. (P072)
15. Select organization schemes by user knowledge and task: exact schemes support known-item access, ambiguous schemes support exploration, and combinations are often necessary. (P073)
16. Use hierarchy, database-oriented structure, hypertext, and tagging as complementary organization structures, selecting each for the part of the environment where its strengths fit. (P074)
17. Use controlled vocabularies and metadata as system glue, shifting from placing documents in one taxonomy to describing them for navigation and retrieval. (P075)
