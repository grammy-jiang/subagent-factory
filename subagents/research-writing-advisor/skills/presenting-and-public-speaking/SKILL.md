# Presenting And Public Speaking

## Purpose

This skill guides preparing and delivering a talk. It checks that the talk is built to rebuild one idea in the audience's minds, structured through signposted speech with a clear throughline, told as a story with an empathetic character and built tension, opened with a hook that teases rather than reveals, kept within about ninety percent of the allotted time, and embodied rather than read — scripted yet improvisational, with unobtrusive notes and a respectful handling of questions.

## When to use

- A talk must be built around one idea and given a signposted, story-driven throughline.
- An opening hook, an explanation, or a format innovation (a prop, an interview, a debate) must engage without giving away the payoff.
- A talk must be rehearsed to fit the time and embodied rather than read from notes or slides.
- Delivery, note support, and question handling must keep the audience connected.

## Procedure

1. When detailed theory is inaccessible in a talk, explain its relation to the field, its project value, and its implications, while candidly discussing relevant limitations and open problems (P044).
2. For notes, prefer unobtrusive support: a comfort backup of notes at the side of the stage, or hand-held cards on a ring, both natural to the audience; avoid smartphones or tablets for regularly-referenced notes, and use confidence monitors only to show slides with minimal notes — never read a full speech off them, because the audience senses being read to and the connection dies (P057).
3. Before building your idea, make clear what it is not, ruling out plausible alternatives so the audience can close in on your meaning; a well-built explanation, made personally relevant and opened with a curious question, can generate real excitement (P073).
4. Convey a talk's hierarchical structure through the linear medium of speech: make explicit how each sentence relates to the last (contrast, cause, exception) using signpost phrases, keep the listener aware of where they are on the throughline's tree, and sequence concepts so understanding builds naturally (P088).
5. Tell a story from the stage with four essentials — an empathetic character, built tension, the right level of detail, and a satisfying resolution — knowing why you are telling it, editing out everything not needed, and never revealing the key twist too early (P089).
6. Treat rebuilding an idea as the speaker's number-one mission — a mental construct the audience can hold, value, and be changed by — inside listeners' minds; a talk succeeds when it plants that idea even if other aspects are flawed (P097).
7. Avoid overrunning: rehearse to confirm the talk fits, watch the clock against a halfway mark, and plan a talk of no more than about 90 percent of your allotted time (finish line = time times 0.9), because overrunning steals from later speakers and audiences have no patience for flab (P098).
8. Build understanding as a hierarchy: introduce dependent concepts one at a time, use metaphors and analogies to connect new facts to the audience's existing mental model, lock each step in with examples, and balance the concepts against the examples and metaphors needed to make them clear (P113).
9. When a future vision could unsettle an audience, make clear why it is worth pursuing and frame it around human values rather than clever technology alone, since humor and a concrete, actionable path reduce the discomfort (P128).
10. Design a technical presentation around one clear message and explain algorithms so their operation remains visible; slides and visual cleverness are supporting means, not the purpose (P134).
11. Protect the whole audience from monopolizing interruptions, and during questions restate each issue, answer everyone briefly and respectfully, admit unknowns, and defer complex discussion when necessary (P148).
12. Build a great talk as both scripted and improvisational, like jazz — a fixed opening, closing, and structure with room for spontaneous moments, always knowing where home is — because a fully improvised talk usually fails and a fully scripted one bores; put every sentence and slide through an essential-and-interesting test, then commit to the mode you feel confident in (P161).
13. Treat true memorization as the ability to deliver the talk while doing another cognitively demanding task and at double speed, so that you live and embody it rather than reciting; use spoken, not written, language — speaking it into a recorder and transcribing that as the first draft — and consider abandoning the script before the last page to speak the conclusion from the heart (P163).
14. Open with a compelling image, video, or object as a strong hook, but do not give away the punchline — tease so the audience wants to come along and save revelations for later — while always mapping where you are going and why; test the opening on friends, treat it as a 10-second then 1-minute attention war, and craft the very first words, which can seize the room (P167).
15. Choose format innovations judiciously: a legitimately relevant prop (practiced in real conditions), an illustrated interview to reduce rambling, a debate to let an idea be challenged, or silence and more-show-less-tell for visual talent; use music cautiously since it can feel like performance or manipulation, and avoid dual presenters unless there is real chemistry (P170).

## Inputs

- The talk under preparation, its one core idea and audience, the time limit, and the delivery or structure concern.
- The reasoning offered for the decision under review: the goal, the audience and venue, the draft or plan in place, and any claim of clarity, rigor, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on research writing and presentation; it does not write the paper, section, slides, or talk for the caller, guarantee acceptance or publication, or rule on the domain-science correctness of the research.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- Deep theoretical machinery is presented with no translation into field relevance, project value, or implications, or limitations and open problems are glossed over as if none exist (P044).
- The speaker reads a full script off a confidence monitor, phone, or tablet, or carries no unobtrusive backup and freezes when memory lapses (P057).
- The idea is asserted directly as "what it is" with no ruling-out of adjacent alternatives, so the audience conflates it with a nearby concept it does not actually match (P073).
- Sentences arrive as a flat, unsignposted list with no contrast/cause/exception markers, leaving listeners unable to say where they are in the argument's tree (P088).
- The narrative has a character and events but no built tension, the wrong grain of detail, or a resolution that fizzles instead of paying off — or the twist is given away early (P089).
- The talk covers many facts, results, or achievements, but afterward the audience cannot state the one idea it was meant to plant (P097).
- Rehearsal is skipped or run only once at a leisurely pace, the halfway mark passes unnoticed, and the talk runs past its full allotted time (P098).
- New concepts pile on top of each other with no connecting metaphor or locking example, so the audience loses the thread partway through (P113).
- A future-facing claim is pitched purely on technical cleverness, with no articulated human value and no concrete, actionable path, leaving the audience unsettled (P128).
- The talk juggles multiple competing messages, or an algorithm's operation is hidden behind a black-box slide while visual cleverness substitutes for clarity (P134).
- One questioner monopolizes the floor, a question goes unrestated and unanswered, or the speaker bluffs on an unknown instead of admitting it and deferring (P148).
- The talk is read verbatim start to end with no room for spontaneity, or attempted fully off the cuff with no fixed opening or closing to return to (P161).
- The speaker can only reproduce the talk at normal pace with full attention, stumbling if interrupted, rushed, or asked to double the pace — a sign it is recited rather than embodied (P163).
- The opening reveals the ending, image, or twist immediately, or the first words spent are throwaway logistics rather than a deliberate hook (P167).
- A format choice (prop, interview, debate, dual presenter, music) is added without rehearsal in real conditions or without genuine fit, and lands as a distraction or as manipulation (P170).
