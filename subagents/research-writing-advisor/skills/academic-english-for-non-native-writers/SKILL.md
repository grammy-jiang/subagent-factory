# Academic English For Non-Native Writers

## Purpose

This skill guides the grammar and register of academic English for non-native research authors. It covers tense as a rhetorical signal, articles and noun countability, number and unit conventions, modals for future work and recommendations, and voice chosen to signal evidence ownership — while keeping the author responsible for correct English and prioritizing the story over the grammar.

## When to use

- A non-native author needs help with tense, articles, countability, number style, or modal choice in a research paper.
- Voice and attribution wording must be chosen so evidence ownership is clear.
- English correctness must be secured (references, a professional editor, an English-speaking coauthor) without letting language overshadow the science.
- A sentence, title, or Discussion passage must be checked for ambiguity between prior work and current findings, or between personal intent and community recommendation.

## Procedure

1. Choose tense as a rhetorical signal: use present simple for warranted general propositions, past simple for completed and bounded studies, and present perfect for prior work that remains relevant to the current research situation (P033).
2. Apply journal-aware scientific form to articles and numbers: choose "a/an" by sound, recognize legitimate specialist article omission, follow digit conventions, keep unit abbreviations unpluralized, and avoid a leading numeral (P079).
3. Take responsibility as the author for submitting correct English — spell and grammar checkers are limited, so keep good references and use a professional editor rather than relying on a friend, and let an English-speaking coauthor own the language — but prioritize the story over the grammar, because an editor can be hired for language but not a scientist for the science, and story structure transcends language (P080).
4. Determine noun countability from the intended contextual meaning, give singular count nouns appropriate determiners, and select indefinite or definite reference according to reader identifiability and contextual uniqueness (P085).
5. Keep grammatically required title articles, use them to distinguish new from specific referents and partial from exhaustive coverage, choose "a/an" by sound, and check field usage when convention and general grammar conflict (P102).
6. Distinguish author-planned future work from community recommendations through commitment-appropriate modals, and formulate recommendations with a grammatical that-clause and base verb (P126).
7. Choose Discussion voice from journal practice but use first-person possessives, named authors, citations, or study labels wherever passive wording would blur attribution (P137).
8. Signal evidence ownership at the sentence start and combine tense, named authors, citations, and study-display references; then state the substantive relationship when moving between prior and current work (P169).

## Inputs

- The passage and its grammatical or register question, the field's usage conventions, and the author's language situation.
- The reasoning offered for the decision under review: the goal, the audience and venue, the draft or plan in place, and any claim of clarity, rigor, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on research writing and presentation; it does not write the paper, section, slides, or talk for the caller, guarantee acceptance or publication, or rule on the domain-science correctness of the research.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- Present-simple, past-simple, and present-perfect are mixed inconsistently across a passage about the same prior work, so the reader cannot tell whether it is treated as accepted fact, a bounded completed study, or work still relevant now (P033).
- A sentence opens with a bare numeral, a unit abbreviation is pluralized, or an article is added or dropped against journal convention and normal sound-based "a/an" choice (P079).
- The draft's English correctness rests solely on an automatic checker or a non-expert friend, with no professional editor or English-speaking coauthor accountable for the language — or, conversely, so much effort goes into wording that the underlying story and argument structure is left thin (P080).
- An indefinite article is used for a noun the reader can already identify, "the" is used before a noun not yet established as specific, or a plural or uncountable noun is given a determiner meant for singular count nouns (P085).
- A title drops a grammatically required article, or its article use makes the title read as covering all instances when only some are meant (or the reverse) (P102).
- A personal intention for future work is phrased with a modal that reads as a directive to the field, a field-level recommendation is phrased as a personal plan, or a recommendation clause skips the grammatical that + base-verb form (e.g., "we recommend to use") (P126).
- A Discussion sentence uses a passive construction that leaves it unclear whether the finding, method, or claim belongs to the current authors or to a cited study (P137).
- A sentence opens with an unattributed claim or a bare pronoun ("It shows...", "They found...") so the reader cannot tell at first read whether it reports prior work or the current study, or the sentence never states how the two relate (agree, extend, contradict) (P169).
