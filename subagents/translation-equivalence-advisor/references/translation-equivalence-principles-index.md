# Translation Equivalence — Principles Index

Every promoted principle (P001–P116), grouped by the skill that owns it. Each principle resolves into `principles/principles.yaml`; its `derived_from_claims` resolve into `analysis/claims.jsonl`.

## Word-Level Non-Equivalence and Strategies

- **P001** — Diagnose the specific type of word-level non-equivalence — culture-specific concept, unlexicalized concept, semantic complexity, differing meaning distinctions, a missing superordinate or hyponym, differing physical or interpersonal perspective, a missing form, differing frequency of a form, or a loan word or false friend — but do not map a type to a strategy in a fixed one-to-one way, since context and purpose favour some strategies and rule out others..
- **P011** — Distinguish the propositional, expressive, presupposed, and evoked meanings of a word or utterance when assessing a rendering, recognising that a charge of inaccuracy usually concerns propositional meaning while expressive meaning is separable, gradable, and truth-neutral..
- **P012** — Use the structure of semantic fields, comparing an item against the alternatives it contrasts with, to assess its value and to fill target-language gaps by moving up the superordinate/hyponym hierarchy, while remembering that languages encode only environmentally relevant distinctions and that the field concept does not fit all words..
- **P037** — Never assume a one-to-one correspondence between words and meanings across languages.
- **P059** — Translate with a loan word, optionally explained once so it can then stand alone, for culture-specific items, modern concepts, and buzz words, weighing that loan words may be chosen for evoked prestige and that the target society's norms bound how freely they may be used..
- **P060** — Translate by paraphrase — a related word for a differently-lexicalized concept, or unrelated words for an unlexicalized or semantically complex one — accepting that paraphrase gives high propositional precision but cannot carry expressive, evoked, or associative meaning and is cumbersome..
- **P080** — When a target equivalent carries the wrong expressive load, prefer a more neutral word and, if needed, restore expressive meaning with a modifier, because it is easier to add expressive meaning than to subtract it, so the hardest case is a target equivalent more emotionally loaded than the source..
- **P081** — Use cultural substitution — replacing a culture-specific item with a target item of similar impact but different propositional meaning — when the item works chiefly for expressive value, gauging its acceptability by the commissioner's licence, the translation's purpose, and the target community's tolerance for departing from propositional meaning..
- **P082** — Use omission only as a last resort, when an item is not vital to the text and rendering it would distract the reader, or to drop expressions that betray a source-text orientation irrelevant to a different target audience, accepting that some loss of meaning is inevitable..
- **P095** — Do not erase culturally embedded source items merely to sound natural; retain, describe, or annotate them when the foreign setting is part of the message..
- **P102** — Resolve non-equivalence by context rather than by absolute rules, because the suitable equivalent depends on linguistic and extra-linguistic factors and on how both the source writer and the translator manipulate the linguistic systems..
- **P103** — Before deciding how to handle a non-equivalence, assess its significance in the given context and reproduce only what is focal to the text, rather than trying to render every aspect of every word in isolation..
- **P104** — Translate by a more general word (superordinate) as a broadly applicable strategy for non-equivalence, especially of propositional meaning, since the hierarchical structure of semantic fields is not language-specific..
- **P105** — Translate by illustration when a word lacking a target equivalent denotes a picturable physical entity and space constraints require the text to stay short and concise..
- **P106** — Treat the catalogue of non-equivalence strategies as open rather than exhaustive, and keep studying professional translations to discover further strategies and to weigh each strategy's advantages and disadvantages in context..

## Collocation, Idiom, and Fixed Expression

- **P013** — Treat idioms and fixed expressions as frozen, largely invariant patterns whose meaning, for idioms, cannot be deduced from their parts, and make recognising the idiom the first step, using the cue that the less literal sense an expression makes in context, the more likely it is an idiom..
- **P014** — Apply idiom strategies by fit.
- **P040** — Distinguish grammatical rules (exceptionless restrictions on word classes) from lexical patterning (exception-admitting, individual-word regularities of collocation and idiom), and treat differences in lexical patterning between source and target as a major source of translation difficulty..
- **P042** — Treat collocation, the regular co-occurrence of words, as largely arbitrary and independent of propositional meaning and as reflecting a community's preferred modes of expression rather than any order in the world, so synonyms carry different collocates and cross-language differences can reshape how an event is portrayed..
- **P043** — Judge collocations by what is typical rather than admissible.
- **P044** — Do not assume an idiom has a target equivalent, since how a language expresses a meaning is unpredictable; containing culture-specific items does not itself make an idiom untranslatable, as its meaning and associations do, a similar target idiom may differ in context or connotation, and idiom conventions, acceptable contexts, and frequency differ across languages and registers..
- **P058** — At the interpretation stage, read a word through its collocational pattern rather than substituting a dictionary equivalent, because a collocation's meaning can differ from the sum of its parts and even a formally matching cross-language collocation may mean something different; failing to do so is a common source of inaccuracy..
- **P061** — Recognise marked collocations, unusual combinations that challenge reader expectations for effect, and judge markedness relative to the register's norm, rendering a marked source collocation with a similarly marked target one where the target language and the translation's purpose allow..
- **P063** — Balance typicality against accuracy when rendering a collocation, accepting that some loss, addition, or skewing is unavoidable and judging any change by its significance in context; favour established target patterns that keep the text reading like an original, and do not reproduce source detail that carries no significance for the target reader..
- **P083** — Watch for the two idiom traps, a misleading idiom that also has a plausible literal reading and a source idiom whose target look-alike differs in meaning, and examine the collocational environment of any expression whose meaning is unclear, since idioms carry their own collocational patterns that help reveal them..
- **P107** — For specialized texts, learn which collocates are compatible with a term in both languages, because register-specific collocations extend well beyond terminology and native-speaker intuition alone does not guarantee correct judgement..
- **P108** — When translating a culture-specific collocation that conveys unfamiliar associations, accept an unavoidable partial increase in information, since the unfamiliar association cannot be introduced without hinting to the reader how to interpret it..
- **P109** — Choose an idiom strategy by weighing style, register, and rhetorical effect, not merely whether a similar-meaning target idiom exists, and resist the strong urge to force in a receptor-language idiom however inappropriate..
- **P110** — Use the target language's natural phraseology, its collocations, its own fixed and semi-fixed expressions, and the right level of idiomaticity, to enhance readability and let a translation pass for an original..

## Grammatical Equivalence

- **P009** — Translate voice by function, not form.
- **P015** — Handle grammatical gender by its function.
- **P025** — Expect grammar to constrain the translation like a straitjacket.
- **P026** — When translating pronouns into a language that marks formality (the T/V distinction), gender, or inclusive/exclusive reference, make conscious decisions about participants' relationships and social standing, since these dimensions are often not explicit in English and not recoverable from context, and the familiar/non-familiar dimension reflects tenor, is socially variable, and is among the hardest aspects of grammar to translate..
- **P045** — Recognise that both a language's lexical resources and its grammatical system determine what can be said and how, since each language regularly makes only certain distinctions explicit, such as time, number, gender, shape, visibility, person, proximity, and animacy, and the once-assumed universal categories are not universal..
- **P046** — Do not map tense and aspect mechanically.
- **P055** — Adapt grammar and lexicon according to receptor-language requirements, first classifying terms as ordinary parallels, functional cultural analogues, or culture-specific items..
- **P057** — Always satisfy obligatory receptor-language features, but flag forced additions, specifications, or omissions when the source does not directly support them..
- **P064** — When moving between languages that differ in the grammatical category of number, choose between omitting the number information and encoding it lexically, but do not overspecify number that the target normally leaves implicit unless the context demands it..
- **P084** — Distinguish morphology from syntax and remember the key point for translation.
- **P085** — Render modality by its function rather than its literal form, since certainty, possibility, and obligation take different forms across languages, and investigate how other categories, mood, direct and indirect speech, and causativity, and their meanings are expressed in both languages..

## Thematic and Information Structure

- **P002** — Signal given and new information with the device the target language uses, definiteness, grammatical subordination, cleft or pseudo-cleft structures, or punctuation, rather than transferring the source's, since signalling systems differ across languages and the attenuation model does not fit all; failing to recognise how a structure marks given versus new causes distorting shifts, so preserve each element's status with a natural target structure..
- **P003** — Understand that theme usually coincides with given and rheme with new, and become familiar with at least one Functional Sentence Perspective model, its communicative dynamism, the theme-transition-rheme split, context-dependence, the verb's context-determined thematic status, and the interplay of position, semantics, and context, because it explains free-word-order languages better than the Hallidayan positional account and underlies much translation-problem analysis..
- **P016** — Know the marked-theme devices, fronted theme (mildly marked for adjuncts but strongly for objects, complements, and predicators), predicated theme (an it-cleft presenting its thematic element as new) and identifying theme (a wh-cleft presenting its thematic element as known), both often implying contrast and common in written English because they signal given and new without stress, and distinguish the passive, which thematizes a would-be object without marking it, from fronting, which foregrounds it..
- **P024** — Match thematic markedness by function, not form.
- **P027** — Attend to a text's information dynamics, treating each clause as a message whose interactional organization drives a clean progression of links.
- **P028** — Maintain a discernible thematic method of development in the target text, since the overall choice and ordering of themes rather than any single theme provides orientation and continuity, different text types favour particular themes, and whatever is placed in initial position must cumulatively add up to a method of development or the text feels disjointed..
- **P029** — Order information given-before-new, the hearer-oriented, context-dependent default that aids comprehension and underlies end-focus and end-weight, and resolve clashes between end-weight and a basic target grammatical principle, such as English subject-before-predicate, rather than letting a heavy-subject, light-verb result stand, for instance by switching passive to active and inserting a subject..
- **P030** — Handle marked information focus by the target's means.
- **P047** — Analyse each clause as a theme, what it is about placed in initial position and orienting back and forward, plus a rheme, what is said about it and carrying the key information; theme-rheme is hierarchical and text-based, accounting for a sequence's acceptability in context rather than its grammaticality, so a clause-by-clause grammatical text can still be thematically ill-formed..
- **P048** — Draw on the strategies for resolving tension between syntax and information flow.
- **P065** — Read markedness as meaning.
- **P066** — Recognise the limits of the Hallidayan initial-position view of theme, its partial circularity and poor fit for verb-initial and topic-prominent languages, and handle topic-prominent languages such as Chinese, Japanese, and Korean carefully, since their always-definite, clause-spanning topic sets a framework and has no adequate English packaging device, so rendering it with an expression like as for wrongly adds contrastiveness and tends to be overused into English..
- **P067** — Judge givenness by what the speaker assumes is in the addressee's consciousness, and reintroduce a previously mentioned element as new, for example with an indefinite form, after enough intervening text or a marked scene change, a decision that matters when abridging, expanding, or rewriting, while remembering that givenness is assigned by the speaker and may be presented without warrant for rhetorical effect..
- **P086** — Treat word order as significant beyond grammar.
- **P088** — Preserve the source's thematic patterning when its elements sit naturally in target theme position, but when preserving it would distort the target, abandon it and give the target its own method of development and continuity, since a pattern natural in one language, such as an ego-centred series of I themes, may need a different one, such as process-centred thematized verbs, in another..
- **P089** — Recognise that word order serves syntactic, semantic, and communicative functions at once and that languages prioritise them differently by how fixed their order is, English leading with syntax and free-order languages following communicative perspective, so translating between them necessarily skews information flow to some degree..
- **P090** — Present the target text from a similar perspective to the source where possible, but where word-order restrictions, end-weight, or natural phraseology prevent it, give the target text its own thematic organization rather than copying the source's every clause, ensuring it reads naturally, does not distort the original's information structure, preserves any special emphasis of marked structures where possible, and maintains a coherent point of view in its own right..

## Cohesion and Texture

- **P004** — Treat cohesion as the surface network of lexical and grammatical relations, reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, and lexical cohesion, that links a text's parts, and rework it to each language's own patterns for conveying the interrelationships of persons and events, since these patterns cannot be ignored if the translation is to be understood..
- **P017** — Handle conjunction as the marking of a small set of general relations, additive, adversative, causal, temporal, and continuative, whose realization and frequency vary sharply across languages, adding or thinning typical target conjunctions to suit its norms, such as English small chunks with varied conjunctions versus Arabic large chunks with few general ones, while balancing the naturalness of typical conjunctions against the accuracy of precise ones..
- **P018** — Accept that lexical-cohesion networks cannot be reproduced identically, since every strategy such as superordinate, paraphrase, or loan word and every grammatical rewording produces different chains and shifts, and idiom-controlled or association-building chains are often sacrificed, but ensure the target text builds sufficient lexical cohesion in its own right, never a random collection of items, using the target language's own tolerance for reiteration so it reads like an original..
- **P031** — Trace participants and entities through reference, the textual identity between expressions, most often pronouns, noting that a language's number and gender pronoun distinctions and its reliance on situational reference and world-knowledge co-reference shape its reference chains, which range on a continuum from full repetition through synonym and superordinate to pronoun, so rework reference to the target's preferred pattern..
- **P038** — Do not transfer the source text's cohesive devices; rework the methods of establishing links to the target language's textual norms, because each language's grammar and each genre's conventions favour different devices, so Arabic agreement makes pronominal reference safe across clauses where English prefers lexical repetition, and legal texts repeat even where a pronoun would be unambiguous..
- **P049** — Adjust conjunction and chunking carefully, because conjunction is genre-specific and reflects a text's rhetoric and line of argument so that changing it can change content, and rechunk a text only for a clear reason, source chunks mismatched to target norms or a different target audience, recognising that a more or less conjunctive style can be equally appropriate to its context..
- **P068** — Rework reference patterns to the target language's norms, since anaphora varies within and across languages and by text type, English leaning on pronouns, Hebrew and Brazilian Portuguese on names and repetition, and Japanese and Chinese on subject omission, so for example turn a subject-omitting chain into an English name-then-pronoun chain rather than reproducing inferable chains..
- **P069** — Remember that the five cohesive devices exist in many languages but with different preferences, frequencies, and combinations, that cohesion is also carried by tense continuity, stylistic consistency, and punctuation so that unmotivated style shifts disrupt it, and that translation tends to raise explicitness, explicitation being a likely near-universal of language mediation..
- **P087** — Analyse a text through its levels of connection, thematic and information structures for topic development, cohesion for surface links, and coherence and implicature for underlying meaning, and use genre and text type, classified by institutional context or by rhetorical mode, to define translation problems and justify strategies..
- **P091** — Decide whether to follow source cohesion patterns or approximate target ones by the translation's purpose and the licence to rechunk, knowing that following source norms minimizes meaning change while deviating from target patterns produces a foreign-sounding text, and take special care when a writer's conjunction use is a stylistic hallmark that is unremarkable in the target language..
- **P112** — Guard coherence at the lexical level, since a single mistranslated polysemous item can shift a text's coherence when the source exploits several of its meanings and the target equivalent carries none of them..

## Pragmatic Equivalence, Coherence, and Implicature

- **P007** — Treat coherence as the reader's judgement, the conceptual relations they build under the surface text, not a property of the text, so cohesive markers alone cannot make a text cohere and a co-reference transparent to the source audience may need an explicit link for the target reader; take account of the target readers' knowledge and their expectations about the world, language, text-type conventions, and social behaviour, since people make sense of new information only through existing knowledge..
- **P019** — Adjust a translation to the target context's realities and ordering conventions, because context supplies the premises for any implicature.
- **P020** — Assess and supply the target reader's background knowledge deliberately.
- **P032** — Use the Co-operative Principle and its four maxims, Quantity, Quality, Relevance, and Manner, as orientation points rather than strict rules.
- **P033** — Treat Grice's specific maxims as culture-relative rather than universal, since Be polite overrides them in some cultures, discourse and rhetoric norms differ (German digression, Arabic repetition-by-assertion, Japanese linkless anecdote) and set the context in which maxims are read, and the maxims reflect English-culture values of sincerity, brevity, and relevance, while relying on the tenable universal that all discourse is cooperative and that implicature itself, not any fixed maxim set, is universal..
- **P050** — Examine anything that would violate the target reader's expectations and adjust it to avoid wrong implicatures, since an unmotivated deviant configuration at any linguistic level blocks meaning, but a target text need not always conform, because a motivated, adequately signalled challenge to the reader's expectations preserves coherence, and a motivated source-text deviation may be transferred if the shared context is enlarged, for example with an explanatory note, so its relevance is explicit..
- **P070** — Aim for explanatory coherence, which justifies the continuity of sense, not merely supplemental coherence that supplies minimal links, and analyse implicature, what a speaker implies beyond the literal signalled conventionally through conjunctions or presupposing structures or non-conventionally, keeping it distinct from conventional idiomatic meaning..
- **P071** — Handle implicature carefully in translation, since implicatures are often indeterminate and a rendering can eliminate a source interpretation or introduce a new one.
- **P072** — Identify each reference well enough to interpret the associations it triggers, not just who or what it is, since an unknown name or culture-specific item breaks continuity and obscures relevance, a cultural substitute must actually share the referent's associations, and a pronoun such as we can turn ambiguous when the text's perspective is unclear..
- **P073** — Weigh politeness and taboo against accuracy, since in some contexts being polite outranks being accurate and a translator may omit or replace text that would offend on a taboo subject, judge for the target culture what counts as more informative than required, and renegotiate the balance of Relevance and Quantity when rewriting, editing, summarizing, or expanding for a different readership..
- **P092** — Respect the target reader's sense of what is socially and textually appropriate, the context for Be polite, including conventions like the expected calendar, and adjust modes of address, titles, name combinations, and terms of affection, to the target's norms to avoid unintended implicatures, except where a deliberate misuse of an address rule carries meaning that is hard to transfer..
- **P093** — Minimize discrepancies between the source text's model of the world and the target reader's, calibrating how much to intervene by the reader's assessed knowledge, since more shared knowledge and world-model harmony mean less intervention and a more invisible translator, and by where the translator's loyalties lie, while balancing fulfilling the reader's expectations against maintaining their interest, so as not to over-explain and leave the reader nothing to do..
- **P113** — Read utterances by their implicit function, not only their form, using speech-act theory alongside implicature.

## Dynamic and Formal Equivalence and Receptor Response

- **P008** — In dynamic equivalence, make formal adjustments where literary form, idioms, or culturally embedded emotive meanings would otherwise block or distort receptor understanding..
- **P021** — Choose translation tactics from the message type, communicative purpose, and intended audience rather than from a single universal method..
- **P022** — Use formal-equivalence or gloss strategies when readers need close access to source form, terminology, structure, and source-cultural context..
- **P023** — For culturally nonmatching referents, choose deliberately among formal term plus note, functional equivalent, borrowed term with classifier, and receptor-language description..
- **P034** — Use dynamic-equivalence strategies when the target task is to reproduce the receptor effect or relationship to the message rather than to display source form..
- **P035** — Evaluate translation adequacy by sense, source spirit and manner, natural receptor expression, and similar audience response, while accepting multiple valid solutions..
- **P036** — For dynamic equivalence, test naturalness across receptor language and culture, the local message context, and the intended audience while preserving source meaning..
- **P052** — Prefer receptor-language idioms and expressions that make the message meaningful for the audience, especially when bare intelligibility would under-communicate the intended force..
- **P053** — Calibrate vocabulary, style, explanation, and explicitness to the audience’s decoding capacity, experience, and interests..
- **P054** — Diagnose linguistic and cultural distance before translating, watching for false friends and for cultural gaps that may matter more than grammar..
- **P056** — Preserve the source’s total stylistic impression by adapting style norms, emotional tone, authorial stance, and character voice for receptor-language effect..
- **P074** — When retaining source forms, idioms, or added target-language words in a formal translation, make the support visible through notes or markers when needed for comprehension..
- **P094** — Use concordant terminology only where it helps readers track important source terms; relax it when consistency produces unnatural or meaningless receptor text..
- **P097** — Infer both the original audience and the receptor audience before targeting equivalent response; do not assume either audience is the general public..
- **P098** — Use expansion and redundancy to support equivalent communication load and decoding rate, but scope additions to the message, audience, setting, and publication purpose..
- **P100** — Use back-translation only to expose a target text's morphological, syntactic, or lexical structure, remembering it is a theoretically unsound compromise that never reproduces the original's meaning..
- **P115** — Account for cultural expectations about faithfulness, existing translations, and publication format before deciding how far dynamic equivalence may go..

## Register, Style, and Literary Form

- **P005** — Weight content and form by their communicative function, preserving form more strongly when it carries genre, emotional force, or poetic effect..
- **P041** — Match the translation to the register expectations of its intended receivers along field, tenor, and mode, deciding tenor by the perceived purpose of the translation, unless the purpose is deliberately to give the reader a flavour of the source culture..
- **P075** — Check register and discourse fit locally.
- **P076** — Translate poetry as receptor-language poetry that elicits comparable feeling, rather than as word-for-word formal correspondence..
- **P077** — When music is preserved, allow metrical adaptation, theme rearrangement, omission, addition, or alteration, but keep lyric wording fully natural..
- **P099** — Treat source sound effects as effects to approximate opportunistically, not as forms that can normally be preserved with close similarity..
- **P114** — Treat literary form and delivery medium as real constraints on how much dynamic equivalence a translation can achieve..
- **P116** — For song translation, fit words to the musical vehicle first, including phrase length, syllable count, stress, rhyme, and singable vowel quality..

## Text-Level Approach and the Limits of Equivalence

- **P006** — Aim for equivalence at the level of the whole text rather than the word or phrase.
- **P010** — Base translation decisions on a systematic understanding of language and reflect on them deliberately rather than relying on intuition or an unexplained flair, since theoretical knowledge only delivers its value (lower risk, readiness for the unpredictable, confident choices) when grounded in practical experience..
- **P039** — Analyse a text at both the level of its individual forms and the level of the whole discourse, because the linguistic levels are not discrete and a word- or grammar-level choice is shaped by, and has implications for, the whole text; both bottom-up and top-down analysis are valid..
- **P051** — Treat translation as an interpretive approximation.
- **P062** — Guard against source-language interference.
- **P078** — Treat equivalence as relative and only partially achievable rather than an absolute standard, because it is shaped by linguistic and cultural factors; equivalence is a convenient working term, not a fixed theoretical target..
- **P079** — Evaluate a translation as a set of strengths and weaknesses open to improvement rather than as simply good or bad, and treat translation strategies as options observed in real practice, not preconceived correct solutions to apply mechanically..
- **P096** — Avoid solving ambiguity or historical distance with phrasing that becomes technical, legalistic, anachronistic, or artificially archaic..
- **P101** — Perceive the meaning of words and utterances precisely in their specific context before rendering them, despite the inherently blurred and negotiable edges of word meaning..
- **P111** — Judge whether a deviant configuration is justified from the reader's point of view, not the translator's.
