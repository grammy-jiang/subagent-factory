# Multimedia And E-Learning Design

## Purpose

This skill applies the evidence on how people learn from words and pictures. It analyses a message by how the learner processes it — verbal versus pictorial presentation mode, auditory versus visual sensory modality — rather than by counting delivery devices; designs against dual channels, limited capacity, and active processing; and routes words away from the visual channel by preferring narration to concurrent on-screen text when a graphic must be processed at the same time in a system-paced presentation. It strips seductive details, places printed words next to the graphic part they describe, eliminates split attention wherever the interface creates it, matches graphic type to what is being taught, aims for a schematic rather than photographic model, sets practice quantity from criticality and learner time cost, diagnoses whether the problem is extraneous, essential, or generative processing before prescribing a remedy, declines to design around learning styles or a universally best medium, requires a design principle to be both theory-grounded and evidence-based, and checks learner prior knowledge before applying any principle.

## When to use

- E-learning, slides, animation, video, or any words-plus-graphics material is being designed or reviewed.
- A medium or technology is being chosen, or is being adopted ahead of the pedagogy.
- A design appeals to learning styles, added delivery modes, or added interest as justification.
- Learners must split attention between text, graphic, question, and feedback.

## Procedure

1. When a lesson underperforms, diagnose which of three causes is responsible before choosing a remedy: extraneous processing from confusing instructional design, essential processing from the material's inherent complexity, or generative underuse from an unmotivating communication style, since each cause needs a different fix (P002).
2. Check the design against the three assumptions of the cognitive theory of multimedia learning — dual channels, limited capacity, active processing — and reject any design that implicitly treats the learner as single-channel, unlimited-capacity, and passive (P007).
3. Design graphics for a schematic pictorial model rather than a photographic copy: since the mental representation the learner builds is a structured spatial abstraction, flag visual realism added beyond what the structure requires as buying nothing (P015).
4. Reject any claim that one medium is universally best: research on media utilisation finds no medium superior for all outcomes and all learners, or especially effective for a single subject at a single grade level, so select media for the specific purpose within a single lesson instead (P026).
5. Design for a small working memory: check that no more than about five to seven chunks per channel are held at once, and remember that what the learner retains is a selection of portions, not a copy, of the presentation (P038).
6. Treat justification by individual learning styles with caution, since research has identified few intellectual or personality characteristics reliably tied to success with a specific form or medium; allow instead that a material may work better because it matches the learner's entering capabilities, more fully provides the events that individual needs, or offers design features such as small versus large steps, inductive versus deductive, or concrete versus abstract (P040).
7. Before applying any design principle, check learner prior knowledge: several principles help low-knowledge learners more than high-knowledge learners, so a principle applied blind to expertise level can be ineffective or counterproductive (P042).
8. Before recommending a design principle, require it to meet both criteria — theory-grounded (derived from an explicit account of how people learn) and evidence-based (supported by empirical research) — and state which criterion is missing when only one holds (P092).
9. Do not add seductive details — interesting but irrelevant sounds, pictures, or words — to counter boredom or dropout: they reliably reduce learning, and making them more interesting makes learning worse (P093).
10. Match graphic type to what is being taught: use relational graphics for quantitative relationships, transformational graphics for change over time, organizational graphics for qualitative structure, and interpretive graphics for invisible relationships (P124).
11. Set practice quantity from task criticality and the automaticity requirement, weighing learner time — extra practice can cost lower-ability learners 75 percent more time for a small gain — and avoid over-learning where it is not needed (P155).
12. Analyse a multimedia message by how the learner processes it — presentation mode (verbal versus pictorial) and sensory modality (auditory versus visual) — rather than by how many delivery devices it uses, and reject delivery-device counting as a design frame (P156).
13. When words accompany a graphic, animation, or video in a system-paced presentation, route the words away from the visual channel: prefer narration over concurrent onscreen text, since printed and onscreen text enters through the eyes and competes with the graphic for the same limited channel, while pictures load the visual/pictorial channel and spoken words the separate auditory/verbal channel (P157).
14. Treat the design problem as guiding appropriate cognitive processing without exceeding working-memory capacity, remembering that the three demands — extraneous, essential, and generative processing — all draw on the same limited pool, so weigh any added material, guidance, or interactivity against that shared budget (P158).
15. Apply the first contiguity principle: place printed words next to the part of the graphic they describe, using a pointing line, rather than putting them in a caption, legend, or separate text block (P159).
16. Eliminate split attention wherever it appears in the interface: scrolling screens that separate text from graphic, feedback shown on a screen apart from the question and the learner's answer, linked windows that cover what they explain, and exercise directions placed away from the work area (P160).

## Inputs

- The instructional material or interface under review, its learners' expertise, and the medium, pacing, and control the design assumes.
- The reasoning offered for the design decision under review: the intended learning, the learners and setting, the evidence already gathered, and any claim of alignment, effectiveness, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on instructional design; it does not build the course, materials, or item bank for the caller, teach or grade learners, or certify a programme or its subject-matter content.

## Anti-patterns to flag

The following is the curated, highest-impact subset of failure modes for this skill; the full set of 16 principles is covered in ## Procedure.

- Slides or narration carry interesting but irrelevant sounds, pictures, or asides added to fight boredom or dropout, when seductive details reliably reduce learning and making them more interesting makes learning worse (P093).
- A graphic is accompanied by scrolling or paragraph-length onscreen text that duplicates the narration in a system-paced presentation, loading the same visual channel with both the graphic and the words instead of routing the words to narration (P157).
- Text describing a graphic's parts sits in a caption, legend, or separate text block instead of next to the part it describes with a pointing line, violating the first contiguity principle (P159).
- The interface separates text from its graphic across a scroll, shows feedback on a different screen from the question and the learner's answer, covers material with a linked window, or places exercise directions away from the work area, forcing the learner to split attention (P160).
- A single screen or slide presents more separate items than a small working memory can hold, exceeding the roughly five-to-seven chunk span instead of chunking or pacing the material (P038).
- A graphic's type does not match what is being taught, such as an organizational diagram used to show a quantitative relationship or a transformational sequence used to show structure, instead of choosing relational, transformational, organizational, or interpretive graphics for the corresponding content (P124).
- A graphic or simulation is built at a highly realistic, near-photographic fidelity when the instructional goal is conceptual understanding, adding visual realism beyond what the structure requires (P015).
- Practice repetitions are set without regard to task criticality or the automaticity requirement, over-learning a skill that does not need it and burning learner time, since extra practice can cost lower-ability learners 75 percent more time for a small gain (P155).
