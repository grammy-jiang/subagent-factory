# Learning Outcomes And Taxonomy

## Purpose

This skill classifies and levels what learners are meant to achieve. It resolves broad objective language into the specific knowledge subtype and cognitive process the performance actually requires, words objectives by intended cognition rather than by observable response format, separates remembering from understanding and refuses retention evidence as proof a design worked, distinguishes routine execution from adaptive implementation, and treats analysis, evaluation, and creation as either transfer targets or means to simpler learning. It uses taxonomy as an adaptable analytic heuristic rather than a literal reality or a teaching prescription, classifies a task for a stated learner group and prior-experience state, analyses intellectual performance at its actual transfer level, and resolves conflicting signals by comparing intended wording, enacted activity, assessed content, and scoring criteria.

## When to use

- An objective, outcome statement, or standard is too broad to design or assess against.
- A design must decide the cognitive level it is actually targeting, or whether a claimed higher-order verb survives contact with the enacted task.
- A taxonomy classification is disputed, or is being applied as a rule rather than a heuristic.
- Transfer-level or understanding outcomes must be stated in a form that can be taught, observed, and assessed with sufficiently novel material.

## Procedure

1. Use taxonomy as an adaptable analytic heuristic, not a literal reality, source of educational aims, guaranteed solution, or unique teaching prescription (P001).
2. Use the objective to set up the assessment situation directly (P031).
3. Set stage-appropriate levels of understanding and use SOLO to distinguish relevant detail from integration, coherent relation, and transfer to broader domains (P076).
4. Resolve broad objective language into the specific knowledge subtype and cognitive process that successful learner performance actually requires (P077).
5. Use analyze, evaluate, and create either as explicit transfer objectives or as means to simpler learning, and allow them to initiate or iterate with other processes rather than imposing a fixed sequence (P105).
6. Word objectives in terms of intended cognition rather than observable response format (P115).
7. Distinguish routine execution from adaptive implementation; for the latter, require recognition of the problem type, selection or construction of a fitting method, adaptation, and execution (P141).
8. Use analysis to select relevant elements, organize their relation to the whole, and infer underlying purpose; represent the resulting structure explicitly when useful (P142).
9. Distinguish remembering from understanding in every objective and every test (P153).
10. To assess transfer or cognition beyond remembering, use sufficiently novel material to prevent memorized reproduction without adding irrelevant knowledge demands (P161).
11. Build and assess conceptual understanding through integration with prior frameworks and complementary transformations such as interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, and summarizing (P170).
12. In unit review, compare intended wording, enacted activities, assessment content, and scoring criteria, using specific knowledge subtypes and processes to resolve conflicting signals (P173).
13. Write course-specific goals as concrete statements of what students will be able to do after the course, with at least one goal under each taxonomy category the course claims, rather than leaving goals in generic form (P179).
14. Operationalize cognitive categories with definitions, examples, assessment tasks, and instructional activities, and use disputed classifications to improve design rather than to win a labeling contest (P182).
15. Classify a task for a specified learner group and prior-experience state (P184).
16. Analyze intellectual performance at its actual transfer level (P188).

## Inputs

- The objectives or outcome statements under discussion, the tasks and scoring criteria that enact them, and the learner group and its prior experience.
- The reasoning offered for the design decision under review: the intended learning, the learners and setting, the evidence already gathered, and any claim of alignment, effectiveness, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on instructional design; it does not build the course, materials, or item bank for the caller, teach or grade learners, or certify a programme or its subject-matter content.
