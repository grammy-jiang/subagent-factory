# Learning Outcomes And Taxonomy

## Purpose

This skill classifies and levels what learners are meant to achieve. It resolves broad objective language into the specific knowledge subtype and cognitive process the performance actually requires, words objectives by intended cognition rather than by observable response format, separates remembering from understanding and refuses retention evidence alone as proof a design worked, distinguishes routine execution from adaptive implementation, and treats analysis, evaluation, and creation as either transfer targets or means to simpler learning. It uses taxonomy as an adaptable analytic heuristic rather than a literal reality or a teaching prescription, classifies a task for a stated learner group and prior-experience state, analyses intellectual performance at its actual transfer level, and resolves conflicting signals by comparing intended wording, enacted activity, assessed content, and scoring criteria.

## When to use

- An objective, outcome statement, or standard uses vague or generic wording that must be resolved into a specific knowledge subtype and cognitive-process level before it can be taught or assessed against.
- An objective's verb names only a response format (list, state, write) or a claimed higher-order verb (analyze, evaluate, create) must be checked against what the enacted task actually requires.
- A taxonomy classification is disputed, or is being applied as a fixed rule rather than an adaptable heuristic.
- Intended wording, enacted activity, assessment content, and scoring criteria give conflicting signals about what level of learning is actually being targeted or measured, and need to be reconciled.

## Procedure

1. Use taxonomy as an adaptable analytic heuristic, not a literal reality, source of educational aims, guaranteed solution, or unique teaching prescription (P001).
2. Use the objective to set up the assessment situation directly, since it describes what must be observed to verify the learning and can equally ground a teacher-made test or a student self-test, and treat objectives as both the guideline for developing instruction and the basis for the measures that determine whether they were reached (P031).
3. Set stage-appropriate levels of understanding and use SOLO to distinguish relevant detail from integration, coherent relation, and transfer to broader domains (P076).
4. Resolve broad objective language into the specific knowledge subtype and cognitive process that successful learner performance actually requires (P077).
5. Use analyze, evaluate, and create either as explicit transfer objectives or as means to simpler learning, and allow them to initiate or iterate with other processes rather than imposing a fixed sequence (P105).
6. Word objectives in terms of intended cognition rather than observable response format, since verbs such as list, write, or state do not uniquely identify the learning process (P115).
7. Distinguish routine execution from adaptive implementation; for adaptive implementation, require recognition of the problem type, selection or construction of a fitting method, adaptation, and execution (P141).
8. Use analysis to select relevant elements, organize their relation to the whole, and infer underlying purpose; represent the resulting structure explicitly when useful (P142).
9. Distinguish remembering from understanding in every objective and every test: remembering is reproduction or recognition measured by retention tests (quantity of learning), while understanding is use in novel situations measured by transfer tests (quality of learning); do not accept retention evidence alone as evidence that a design worked (P153).
10. To assess transfer or cognition beyond remembering, use sufficiently novel material to prevent memorized reproduction, without adding irrelevant knowledge demands (P161).
11. Build and assess conceptual understanding through integration with prior frameworks and complementary transformations such as interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, and summarizing (P170).
12. In unit review, compare intended wording, enacted activities, assessment content, and scoring criteria, using specific knowledge subtypes and processes to resolve conflicting signals (P173).
13. Write course-specific goals as concrete statements of what students will be able to do after the course, with at least one goal under each taxonomy category the course claims, rather than leaving goals in generic form (P179).
14. Operationalize cognitive categories with definitions, examples, assessment tasks, and instructional activities, and use disputed classifications to improve design rather than to win a labeling contest (P182).
15. Classify a task for a specified learner group and prior-experience state, since repeated exposure can turn analysis or implementation into execution or recall (P184).
16. Analyze intellectual performance at its actual transfer level, from discrimination through concepts and rules to structured or ill-structured problem solving, and raise the target only when learner needs and application justify it (P188).

## Inputs

- The objectives or outcome statements under discussion, the tasks and scoring criteria that enact them, and the learner group and its prior experience.
- The reasoning offered for the design decision under review: the intended learning, the learners and setting, the evidence already gathered, and any claim of alignment, effectiveness, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on instructional design; it does not build the course, materials, or item bank for the caller, teach or grade learners, or certify a programme or its subject-matter content.

## Anti-patterns to flag

The following is the curated, highest-impact subset of failure modes for this skill; the full set of principles is covered in ## Procedure.

- Taxonomy is applied as a fixed rulebook, with every objective forced into a category rather than the classification being adapted to the actual instructional aim (P001).
- An objective still uses a broad or ambiguous verb or content label after review, so the specific knowledge subtype and cognitive process the performance actually requires cannot be identified (P077).
- An objective's verb names only a response format, such as list, write, or state, leaving the intended cognitive process unstated (P115).
- A design or reported gain is judged effective using only retention or recall scores, with no transfer test administered to check for use in a novel situation (P153).
- An assessment meant to measure understanding, application, analysis, evaluation, or creation reuses material learners have already memorized, so an apparent higher-level score may just be reproduction (P161).
- A task is scored as adaptive mastery when only routine execution was demonstrated, with no unfamiliar-problem step requiring recognition of the problem type, selection or construction of a fitting method, adaptation, and execution (P141).
- Intended objective wording, the enacted activity, the assessment content, and the scoring criteria diverge from one another, and the conflict is left unresolved instead of being reconciled against the specific knowledge subtype and process intended (P173).
- Course goals remain generic topic coverage, with no concrete "students will be able to do X" statement written under each taxonomy category the course claims (P179).
