# Instructional Strategy And Events

## Purpose

This skill selects and sequences what actually happens in instruction, derived from the goal, the analyses, the objectives, and the assessments rather than from habit. It treats the nine events as a design checklist rather than a mandatory script, prescribes a method together with the situation it fits, and sequences like a zoom lens — an epitome of the whole, then elaboration of one part, then synthesis back to the whole. It sequences by prerequisite and manageable progression with entry checks and pretests, and matches technique to outcome type: concepts through varied instances and diagnostic non-instances with irrelevant shortcut cues removed, intellectual skills through activated prerequisites and progressive transfer cases, verbal information through organised prior-knowledge links, attitudes through credible models and delivered consequences, motor skills through part and whole practice. It scaffolds generative and complex performance from supported to independent work, fades prompts so contiguity is established with the intended cue, keeps every practice and test instance new, uses spaced retrieval rather than re-presentation, makes training conditions resemble retrieval conditions, builds strategy flexibility across multiple valid methods, and budgets time across the whole sequence.

## When to use

- The activities, examples, practice, and sequence of a unit or course must be chosen or reviewed.
- Different outcome types — concepts, intellectual skills, verbal information, attitudes, motor skills — need matching techniques.
- Learners must be scaffolded from supported work to independent production, or prerequisites are unmet.
- A recommended technique needs the situation, conditions, and time budget that make it the right one stated alongside it.

## Procedure

### The nine-events-and-checklist frame

1. Treat the nine events of instruction as a design checklist to weigh for any desired learning rather than a mandatory script: each event is a candidate to consider, the order may vary, some events may be omitted, and the form each takes depends on the learning task and the learners (P039).
2. Treat any rule for teaching mental operations as conditional rather than absolute — a method that succeeds under one set of conditions may fail under another — so state the conditions together with the rule whenever recommending or reviewing a principle-based technique (P011).
3. Prescribe a method together with the situation it fits: name which strategy component or model to use and for which pairing of desired outcome with conditions, and flag a recommendation that names a technique without naming its situation as incomplete (P122).
4. Derive the instructional strategy from the goal, the analyses, the objectives, and the assessments, covering motivation, presentation, examples, active practice, feedback, assessment, transfer, and delivery constraints (P117).
5. Evaluate an instructional strategy across preinstruction, presentation, participation, assessment, follow-through, motivation, and operational management, adapting the criteria to the outcomes and the learners being taught (P118).
6. Use current motivation, outcome-type, and strategy principles as explicit design-analysis criteria, recording both the strengths and the weaknesses found so the resulting judgment is a defensible synthesis (P146).
7. Plan instruction deliberately at nested levels — lesson inside topic inside course or curriculum — rather than leaving it to moment-to-moment improvisation, since unplanned and undirected learning is judged very likely to leave many individuals unable to derive personal satisfaction from living in society (P025).

### Sequencing and prerequisite order

8. When an assumed prerequisite proves unheld, or readiness and prior mastery vary materially across learners, add prerequisite instruction for the demonstrated deficits, move broadly mastered capabilities into entry checks, and branch each learner to only the teaching or review their profile warrants (P005).
9. Apply the sequencing principle proper to each outcome type: precede a new intellectual skill by mastery of its subordinate skills; involve recall of relevant intellectual skills in problem-solving; treat the order of verbal-information subtopics as unimportant while preceding new facts with a meaningful context; establish respect for the source and mastery of the involved intellectual skills before an attitude's choice situation; and for motor skills, practise the critical part skills intensively and the whole skill too, with the executive subroutine learned first (P028).
10. When sequencing the content of a whole course, sequence like a zoom lens: open with a wide-angle epitome showing the major parts and their relationships without detail, zoom in on one part, then zoom back out to review the whole and that part's context before zooming in again, repeating the elaborate-summarize-synthesize cycle until the desired complexity is reached (P029).
11. When deriving lesson order from a learning hierarchy, sequence simple-to-complex, teaching subordinate paths before the superordinate ones that contain them, while recognizing that the order between independent hierarchies is not prescribed and several such sequences are equally acceptable (P054).
12. When readiness and mastery differ across learners and the delivery system can branch, sequence lessons by prerequisites and manageable progression, then use focused entry checks, authentic whole-task and lesson pretests, embedded feedback assessments, and component-plus-integrated posttests to route instruction and verify mastery (P063).
13. When new learning is assembled from prior concepts and rules, have the component concepts and rules recalled just before the new learning is presented, using a recognition or, better, a recall question — and a chain of such questions — to lead the learner toward the new rule (P024).
14. Build deliberate recall of previously learned content into the lesson whenever the new learning combines previously acquired concepts, rules, or information, because the memory contents present in working memory at the moment of learning are themselves conditions of learning (P059).
15. When preparing the recall and activation events of a lesson, follow the design procedure appropriate to each learner characteristic: stimulate retrieval of prerequisite intellectual skills; provide for retrieval of cognitive strategies where available; stimulate recall of propositions and of the meaningful context or situational schema; activate previously acquired positive attitudes for motivation; and recall the essential part skills for motor learning (P060).
16. When sequencing definitions and criteria within a unit, introduce a concept only after learners have experienced the phenomenon it names, and open the section with a question arising from what they have just observed, so the definition labels something already seen and the new content is motivated by a discrepancy in their own experience (P032).
17. When introducing a module or lesson, replace an abstract statement of objectives with a concrete demonstration of the terminal capability the learner will acquire, since stated objectives are often too abstract to communicate meaningfully and a demonstration also previews, motivates, and synthesizes (P035).

### Matching technique to outcome type

18. When assessing a defined-concept objective, require the concept to be demonstrated rather than stated: classifying an instance in accordance with the definition is a different mental process from reciting the definition, and asking what a term means is always open to the learner repeating a verbalisation without knowing the meaning (P027).
19. When the objective is a concrete concept learners must generalize over time, teach it by presenting instances that vary widely in their non-relevant characteristics together with negative instances, so correct performance is shown by both selecting members and rejecting non-members, and ensure first that the underlying discriminations are recallable (P037).
20. When learners must classify by a defining characteristic, teach the classification with coherent learner-relevant examples, diagnostic nonexamples, removal of irrelevant shortcut cues, and a rule-based explanation for every judgment (P147).
21. When learners must discriminate defining features rather than surface cues, teach and assess multiattribute concepts with closely matched examples and nonexamples, immediate error review, and new but strategy-parallel items across pretest, practice, and posttest (P150).
22. When designing or evaluating concept, rule, procedure, or problem-solving instruction, teach intellectual skills through activated prerequisites, explicit prior-to-new links, diagnostic examples and nonexamples, application steps and quality criteria, progressive transfer cases, authentic application practice, and explanatory feedback (P074).
23. When designing or evaluating recall-oriented instruction, teach verbal information in a relevant, prior-knowledge-linked organization and rehearse meaningful recall cues, using logical mnemonics only when meaningful elaboration is insufficient (P189).
24. When instruction seeks to influence a choice or disposition, make the desired and undesired choices and their consequences explicit, use relatable credible models, and ensure feedback delivers the promised consequence directly or vicariously (P190).
25. When instructional time is limited and conceptual recognition or use is the objective, or learners must eventually perform foundational facts or procedures independently, build fluency from conceptual relationships and progressively faded representations, prioritizing meaning before formal labels while preserving the labels needed for later communication (P143).

### Scaffolding, practice, and retrieval

26. When teaching a strategy or routine, prompt each example with attention-focusing information linking its specific attributes to the labels the definition uses, then fade that prompting in the later stages, because constantly available help lets learners stop studying examples and gives an illusion of understanding, while fading is what produces the mental effort real understanding requires (P019).
27. When the stimulus a learner practises against differs from the one the objective names, practise against the stimulus named in the objective: where a model or prompt stands between the intended cue and the response, fade it in stages so contiguity is established with the intended cue rather than the prompt (P051).
28. When planning review for an information objective, make every practice and test instance new — different from those used in teaching — or the item measures recall rather than the intended performance, and require a paraphrase rather than a verbatim restatement when the learner must state a generality (P052).
29. When the learner must attend to particular features of the material, delineate the distinctive features of every stimulus presented — highlighting features of diagrams, using headings and emphasis in text, varying tone and volume in speech — and for intellectual skills present varied examples of the concept, rule, or problem (P053).
30. When planning review for retention, or when intellectual skills or a learned performance must be recalled after instruction, employ repetition as spaced occasions for the learner to recall what was learned rather than as mere re-presentation, since retrieval processes are apparently the most important factor in remembering both isolated facts and larger bodies of information (P036).
31. When learners are moving from recognition to open production, scaffold generative performance from transforming supplied examples to independent creation, support execution with concise criteria, and show multiple representative solutions when several answers are valid (P086).
32. When learners are acquiring a multistep process containing embedded analytic judgment, scaffold the complex procedure from reduced common material to independent projects, modeling both actions and decisions, monitoring guided practice, and remediating before release (P185).
33. When multiple strategies can produce the same outcome and adaptive choice is an objective, develop strategy flexibility by having learners apply, explain, and compare multiple valid methods, and diagnose actual method use rather than inferring it from answers or speed alone (P181).
34. When designing practice or examples intended to change on-the-job performance or transfer to a specific work environment, apply encoding specificity and transfer-appropriate interaction: make training conditions resemble retrieval conditions, practising on the same system, cases, and interfaces used on the job (P123).

### Time budgeting

35. When planning sessions and support for mediated or individualized instruction, budget time for motivation, readiness checks, instruction, practice, integration, and assessment, combining individualized delivery with prepared human support where learner readiness varies (P180).

## Inputs

- The objectives and assessments already fixed, the learners' entry performance, and the activities, examples, and practice currently planned.
- The reasoning offered for the design decision under review: the intended learning, the learners and setting, the evidence already gathered, and any claim of alignment, effectiveness, or readiness made.

## Output

Per finding: name the gap and the principle it engages, give the correction, state the residual trade-off or the referral to make, and end with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill advises on instructional design; it does not build the course, materials, or item bank for the caller, teach or grade learners, or certify a programme or its subject-matter content.

## Anti-patterns to flag

The following is a curated, highest-impact subset; the full set of 35 principles this skill draws on is covered in `## Procedure` above.

- A recommendation names a technique but never states the situation or conditions it is meant for, so a reader cannot tell whether it fits their case (P122).
- A technique is applied as a universal rule with no conditions attached, when the same technique can fail under different conditions (P011).
- The nine events are run through as a fixed, mandatory script in a set order rather than weighed as candidates for the actual learning task (P039).
- Instruction proceeds without checking whether an assumed prerequisite is actually held, so a gap only surfaces after learners fail (P005).
- A whole course's content is presented at full complexity up front instead of an epitome-then-elaborate-then-review cycle (P029).
- A lesson teaches a superordinate skill before the subordinate skills it depends on are mastered (P054).
- A concept's formal definition is presented before learners have experienced the phenomenon it names, so nothing in the definition is anchored to anything they have seen (P032).
- A lesson opens with only an abstract statement of objectives and no concrete demonstration of the terminal capability being taught (P035).
- A concept assessment only asks the learner to state or recite the definition rather than classify a new instance against it (P027).
- Concept teaching uses only positive instances, or instances that do not vary on irrelevant characteristics, so learners never practise rejecting a non-member (P037).
- Classification teaching leaves an irrelevant shortcut cue in place that lets learners reach the right answer without applying the actual defining rule (P147).
- Verbal information is presented as an isolated list with no link to prior knowledge and no rehearsed recall cues (P189).
- Worked-example prompting never fades, so learners keep relying on the prompt instead of generating the effort real understanding requires (P019).
- Practice or test items reuse the very instances used in teaching, so the item measures memorized recall rather than the intended performance (P052).
- Practice conditions — the system, cases, or interface used — differ materially from the on-the-job retrieval conditions the training is meant to transfer to (P123).
- A session plan allocates time to content delivery but leaves no budget for readiness checks, integration, or assessment (P180).
