# Accountability dimensions matrix

When someone fails to deliver a *result* they promised, whether to hold them
accountable "depends." Promises that are simple to keep (send the email, finish
the collateral) are absolute — you must hold people to them, because a broken
commitment is contagious. But results that depend on solving genuinely hard
problems require judgment across three dimensions, so that holding people
accountable does not punish the smart risks a company needs people to take.

| Dimension | Question | Lenient end | Strict end |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Seniority** | How experienced is the person? | Junior people are given latitude — they cannot yet forecast accurately | Experienced people are expected to forecast their results accurately and are held to a stricter standard |
| **Degree of difficulty** | How hard was the thing, honestly? | Genuinely hard circumstances (inferior product, mid-quarter recession, unsolved CS problem) warrant more latitude | Routine, well-understood work warrants little latitude |
| **Quality of the decision / bet** | Was the risk a smart one? | A smart, well-reasoned bet that failed is treated forgivingly — you want people taking those | A careless or poorly reasoned risk that failed is treated harshly, regardless of outcome |

## How to use it

- **Score the miss on all three axes before reacting.** A junior person who took
  a smart bet on a genuinely hard problem and missed is a different case from a
  senior person who made a careless bet on routine work and missed — even if the
  numeric shortfall is identical.
- **Separate the bet from the outcome.** Judge the *quality of the decision* at
  the time it was made, not just whether it paid off. Punishing good bets that
  failed teaches people to stop taking the risks the company depends on.
- **Promises vs. results.** Use this matrix only for *results* under
  uncertainty. Simple commitments are held absolutely.
