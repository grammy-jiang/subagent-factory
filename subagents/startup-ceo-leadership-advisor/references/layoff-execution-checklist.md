# Layoff execution checklist

A layoff breaks the trust a CEO spends every day building. The source's claim is
that companies can survive even repeated layoffs *if they lay people off the
right way* — preserving cultural continuity and retaining the best people — and
break if they do it the wrong way. This is the six-step execution sequence.

| # | Step | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Get your own head right** | Accept that the layoff is happening because the *company* failed to hit plan, not because the people are bad. Steady yourself before you face anyone — your demeanour sets the company's. |
| 2 | **Do not delay once the decision is made** | Once you have decided to lay off, move. Delay leaks, breeds rumor, and erodes trust further. |
| 3 | **Be clear about the reason** | Name the real cause. If the cause is financial, say "the company failed to hit its plan and we have to lose excellent people" — do **not** reframe it as cleaning up performance. Conflating company failure with individual performance failure is what breaks trust. Resist board "positive spin." |
| 4 | **Train your managers before the day** | The most important step. Golden rule: **managers lay off their own people** — they cannot delegate it to HR or a more sadistic peer, and you cannot outsource it. Untrained managers sent into this will fail. |
| 5 | **Address the whole company** | Speak to everyone, including those who are staying, before people scatter into one-on-ones. The survivors are watching how their colleagues are treated. |
| 6 | **Be present and visible throughout** | Do not hide in your office. Be in the building, accessible, and visibly carrying the weight of the decision the entire day. |

## Guardrails

- **Distinguish financial failure from performance failure** (Step 3) — this is
  a hard quality bar; getting it wrong damages culture.
- **Preserve dignity** — laid-off people are treated with respect and their
  contributions acknowledged; no disparaging language.
- **Legal review** — severance terms, equity treatment, and written
  communications should be reviewed by counsel before they go out.
