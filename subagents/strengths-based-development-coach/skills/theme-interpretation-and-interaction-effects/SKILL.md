# Theme interpretation and interaction effects

## Purpose

Interpret a person's named StrengthsFinder talent themes in their real context —
what each theme means for how they work, how the themes combine, and where each one
can be over-expressed — without ever inventing or guessing a ranking.

Two rules govern every interpretation:

- **Name the blind spot every time.** Each dominant theme carries a characteristic
  over-expression risk. Presenting a theme as purely positive misrepresents the
  framework and can push the person to over-apply the talent in ways that damage
  relationships, ignore context, or reduce effectiveness (P-005, CL-027). For
  example, strong Command can drive results while bruising relationships; dominant
  Consistency can fixate on uniform process at the expense of the outcome.
- **Never fabricate a ranking.** Only interpret themes that came from a completed,
  timed online assessment. The 20-second instinctive-response format captures the
  most stable, intense natural responses; self-report, manager inference, or coach
  guessing cannot substitute for it (P-007, CL-020). If the caller has not taken the
  assessment, decline to assign themes and direct them to take it first.

## When to use

- A person provides their top-five theme names and wants to understand what they
  mean in practical, role-specific terms.
- You are interpreting themes in any mode — advise, produce, or extract — and need
  the per-theme reading plus the interaction story between themes.
- A caller asks "what does theme X mean for me / for this role?"

Do **not** use this skill to invent themes for someone who has not been assessed —
that is the one situation P-007 forbids. Redirect them to the online assessment.

## Procedure

1. **Confirm the source of the themes.** Verify the themes came from a completed
   online assessment. If they did not, stop and ask the person to take it; do not
   infer rankings (P-007).
2. **Interpret each theme in the person's context.** For every named theme, state
   the core drive of that theme (see `references/34-theme-reference-card.md`) and
   translate it into what it looks like in this person's actual role or situation —
   not a generic definition.
3. **Name the blind spot for each theme.** Alongside the benefit, name at least one
   over-expression risk for that theme — how the same drive can misfire when pushed
   too far (P-005, CL-027). Keep it specific to the theme's drive.
4. **Read the interaction effects.** Look at how the top themes combine. Themes that
   reinforce each other (e.g. a drive theme plus an execution theme) amplify a
   pattern; themes that pull in different directions (e.g. a fast-action theme plus a
   risk-aware theme) create internal tension worth naming. Describe the combined
   signature, not five themes in isolation.
5. **Close with a contextual read, not a label.** Summarise how this particular
   combination tends to show up at work, including where the blind spots overlap and
   compound.

## Inputs

- The person's top-five theme names from a completed online assessment.
- Their current role, goals, or presenting situation, so interpretation is
  contextual rather than generic.
- Confirmation that the themes are assessment-derived (required by P-007).

## Output

- A per-theme interpretation tied to the person's context, each with a named
  blind-spot / over-expression risk.
- An interaction read: how the themes reinforce or tension each other, and the
  combined working signature.
- Where themes are unverified, a refusal to assign rankings plus a pointer to the
  assessment.
